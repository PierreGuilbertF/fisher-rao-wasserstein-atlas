Fisher–Rao and Wasserstein–Otto Geometries of Parametric Probability Families: A Comparative Atlas

Pierre Guilbert · September 22, 2026 · Preprint

Abstract

We compare the Fisher–Rao and Wasserstein–Otto metrics by pulling them back onto the same parametric families of probability densities. After recalling the measure-theoretic and differential-geometric framework, we derive the two ambient constructions and make their relation explicit on the real line. The comparative atlas covers location–dispersion models, multivariate Gaussians, linear and nonlinear Gibbs families, and push-forward families. For each model we specify its parameter and tangent spaces, compute the density differential and both induced metrics, and interpret the resulting geometry through densities, metric indicatrices, and geodesics. Exact paths are distinguished from numerical approximations and from paths constrained to a subfamily. The figures and their Python scripts are available at https://github.com/PierreGuilbertF/fisher-rao-wasserstein-atlas.

1 Introduction

Geometry gives form to mathematical objects and allows us to reason about their deformations. Once a smooth manifold is equipped with a Riemannian metric, each tangent space carries an inner product, and one can define lengths, angles, distances, geodesics and, in finite dimensions, the associated notions of area and volume. These geometric quantities are not only descriptive: they provide tools for moving through and optimizing over nonlinear spaces. This point of view is now standard in many areas of applied mathematics and engineering, including constrained and Riemannian optimization, continuum mechanics and transport, control and robotics, signal and image processing, statistics, and machine learning.

In this manuscript, we apply this geometric viewpoint to the space of strictly positive probability densities. Let EE be a finite-dimensional vector space, equipped when necessary with an inner product, and let 𝒫+(E)={p>0:∫Ep(x)dx=1}.\mathcal{P}_{+}(E) = \left\{ p>0: \int_E p(x)\,dx=1 \right\}. We shall equip this space successively with two classical and complementary geometries: the Fisher–Rao geometry, whose Riemannian interpretation in statistics goes back to Rao [4], and the Wasserstein–Otto geometry, which gives the quadratic Wasserstein distance its formal Riemannian structure on spaces of densities [5]. We use these metrics as complementary geometric descriptions of the same probability families.

If t↦pt∈𝒫+(E)t\mapsto p_t\in\mathcal{P}_{+}(E) is a differentiable curve with p0=pp_0=p, its velocity u=∂tpt|t=0u = \left.\partial_t p_t\right|_{t=0} must preserve the normalization of the density. Hence the admissible tangent directions satisfy Tp𝒫+(E)={u:∫Eu(x)dx=0},T_p\mathcal{P}_{+}(E) = \left\{ u: \int_E u(x)\,dx=0 \right\}, with the additional regularity and integrability assumptions required by the metric under consideration. The same tangent variation may also be described through a velocity field on the underlying space. Indeed, if the density evolves according to the continuity equation ∂tpt+div⁡(ptvt)=0,\partial_t p_t+\operatorname{div}(p_t v_t)=0, then at t=0t=0, u=−div⁡(pv).u=-\operatorname{div}(pv). This second representation will be fundamental for the Wasserstein–Otto geometry, where tangent vectors are interpreted through the motion of probability mass.

The Fisher–Rao metric acts directly on density variations: gpFR(u,w)=∫Eu(x)w(x)p(x)dx.g_p^{\mathrm{FR}}(u,w) = \int_E \frac{u(x)w(x)}{p(x)}\,dx. It measures the relative local variation of the density and does not require a notion of distance between points of EE. By contrast, the Wasserstein–Otto metric is defined through the kinetic cost of transporting the density. Its squared norm can be written formally as [5] ∥u∥p,WO2=infvu=−div⁡(pv)∫E∥v(x)∥2p(x)dx.\|u\|_{p,\mathrm{WO}}^2 = \inf_{\substack{v\\ u=-\operatorname{div}(pv)}} \int_E \|v(x)\|^2 p(x)\,dx. Under the usual regularity assumptions, the minimizing velocity field is a gradient, v=∇ϕuv=\nabla\phi_u [5], where −div⁡(p∇ϕu)=u.-\operatorname{div}\!\left(p\nabla\phi_u\right)=u. The corresponding bilinear form is therefore gpWO(u,w)=∫Ep(x)⟨∇ϕu(x),∇ϕw(x)⟩dx.g_p^{\mathrm{WO}}(u,w) = \int_E p(x) \left\langle \nabla\phi_u(x), \nabla\phi_w(x) \right\rangle \,dx.

Fisher–Rao measures local relative density change, whereas Wasserstein–Otto measures mass displacement through EE. Figure 1 illustrates this contrast on Gaussian densities. Pulling back these ambient metrics can therefore give the same parametric family different lengths, distances, curvature and geodesics.

More precisely, for a smooth parametric family θ↦pθ∈𝒫+(E),\theta\longmapsto p_\theta\in\mathcal{P}_{+}(E), and tangent directions dθ1,dθ2∈TθΘd\theta_1,d\theta_2\in T_\theta\Theta, each ambient metric induces a finite-dimensional metric through gθFR(dθ1,dθ2)=gpθFR(dpθ(dθ1),dpθ(dθ2)),g_\theta^{\mathrm{FR}}(d\theta_1,d\theta_2) = g_{p_\theta}^{\mathrm{FR}} \left( dp_\theta(d\theta_1), dp_\theta(d\theta_2) \right), and gθWO(dθ1,dθ2)=gpθWO(dpθ(dθ1),dpθ(dθ2)).g_\theta^{\mathrm{WO}}(d\theta_1,d\theta_2) = g_{p_\theta}^{\mathrm{WO}} \left( dp_\theta(d\theta_1), dp_\theta(d\theta_2) \right). The statistical model is therefore unchanged, while its geometry depends on the metric through which it is observed.

This expository Atlas gathers classical Fisher–Rao and Wasserstein–Otto constructions in a common pullback framework. Explicit calculations and visualizations compare their induced geometries on one-dimensional location–dispersion models, multivariate Gaussians, linear and nonlinear Gibbs families, and push-forward families. We emphasize the form and interpretation of each metric and, whenever tractable, its distances, geodesics, curvature and gradient dynamics.

3 Framework and notation

The notation follows the conventions used in Guilbert [1]. Readers looking for a more detailed and pedagogical introduction to the objects used below may refer to that work.

3.1 Probability densities

Throughout the paper, EE is a finite-dimensional real vector space equipped with an inner product, most often E=ℝnE=\mathbb{R}^n. We denote its Borel σ\sigma-algebra by ℬ(E)\mathcal{B}(E) and the associated Lebesgue measure by λ\lambda. Unless another measure is specified, all integrals on EE are taken with respect to λ\lambda, and we write dxdx for dλ(x)d\lambda(x).

Every probability measure PP considered below is defined on ℬ(E)\mathcal{B}(E) and is absolutely continuous with respect to λ\lambda. Its density therefore depends on this reference measure and is written p=dPdλ,P(A)=∫Ap(x)dλ(x),A∈ℬ(E).p=\frac{dP}{d\lambda}, \qquad P(A)=\int_A p(x)\,d\lambda(x), \qquad A\in\mathcal{B}(E). We write 𝒫+(E)={p∈L1(E):p>0λ-a.e.,∫Epdλ=1}.\mathcal{P}_{+}(E) = \left\{ p\in L^1(E): p>0\ \lambda\text{-a.e.}, \ \int_E p\,d\lambda=1 \right\}. The set 𝒫+(E)\mathcal{P}_{+}(E) is not open in L1(E)L^1(E), and no Banach manifold structure on it will be needed. We shall nevertheless consider differentiable curves t↦pt∈𝒫+(E)t\mapsto p_t\in\mathcal{P}_{+}(E). Their velocity u=∂tpt|t=0u=\partial_t p_t\big|_{t=0} satisfies ∫Eu(x)dx=0,\int_E u(x)\,dx=0, by differentiation of the normalization. We use Tp𝒫+(E)T_p\mathcal{P}_{+}(E) for the class of such admissible variations at pp, with the additional integrability or regularity required by the metric under consideration.

A finite-dimensional parametric family is a map p:Θ→𝒫+(E),θ↦pθ,p\colon\Theta\longrightarrow\mathcal{P}_{+}(E), \qquad \theta\longmapsto p_\theta, where Θ\Theta is a finite-dimensional smooth manifold, usually an open subset of ℝk\mathbb{R}^k. A direction dθ∈TθΘd\theta\in T_\theta\Theta induces the density variation dpθ(dθ)∈Tpθ𝒫+(E).dp_\theta(d\theta)\in T_{p_\theta}\mathcal{P}_{+}(E).

3.3 Riemannian manifolds

Manifolds are assumed smooth, connected, and finite-dimensional. We denote a manifold by ℳ\mathcal{M} and its tangent space at x∈ℳx\in\mathcal{M} by TxℳT_x\mathcal{M}. 4 Construction of the metrics

4.1 Fisher-Rao

The Fisher–Rao metric is obtained by pulling back the L2L^{2} metric on 𝒫+(E)\mathcal{P}_{+}(E) through the map p↦2pp\mapsto 2\sqrt{p}. For a more detailed account of this construction, we refer the reader to [1]. For the square-root and spherical representation on smooth density spaces, see Khesin, Lenells, Misiołek, and Preston [13] (Sections 3.1–3.3), in the compact-manifold setting.

4.1.1 Fisher–Rao as the pullback of the L2L^{2} inner product

Positive densities live naturally in L1(E)L^{1}(E), which carries no preferred inner product. The square-root map ψ(p)=2p\psi(p)=2\sqrt{p} sends them into L2(E)L^{2}(E), with ⟨f,g⟩L2=∫Efgdx.\left\langle f,g \right\rangle_{L^{2}}=\int_{E} f\,g\,dx. In the finite discrete case, ψ\psi is a smooth immersion of the open simplex onto the positive orthant of the sphere of radius 22, and the pullback of the round metric is exact. Figure 4 makes this identification visible: the Fisher–Rao geometry on the simplex is the round geometry of the sphere, read back through ψ\psi. In the continuous case we keep the same formulas on the directions for which they make sense. Differentiating gives dψp(u)=up,d\psi_{p}(u)=\frac{u}{\sqrt{p}}, defined whenever u/p∈L2(E)u/\sqrt{p}\in L^{2}(E), and therefore gpFR(u,w)=⟨dψp(u),dψp(w)⟩L2=∫Eu(x)w(x)p(x)dx.g_{p}^{\mathrm{FR}}(u,w) = \left\langle d\psi_{p}(u),\,d\psi_{p}(w) \right\rangle_{L^{2}} = \int_{E}\frac{u(x)\,w(x)}{p(x)}\,dx.

The admissible variations at pp are those uu with ∫Eudx=0and∫Eu2pdx<∞.\int_{E} u\,dx=0 \qquad\text{and}\qquad \int_{E}\frac{u^{2}}{p}\,dx<\infty. The first condition is conservation of mass; the second is finiteness of the Fisher–Rao norm. Equipped with gpFRg_{p}^{\mathrm{FR}}, this space is a Hilbert space. We write it Tp𝒫+(E)T_{p}\mathcal{P}_{+}(E) by analogy with a tangent space, without claiming a Banach manifold structure on 𝒫+(E)\mathcal{P}_{+}(E).

On a parametric family θ↦pθ∈𝒫+(E)\theta\mapsto p_{\theta}\in\mathcal{P}_{+}(E), the same construction pulls back to Θ\Theta: gθFR(dθ1,dθ2)=gpθFR(dpθ(dθ1),dpθ(dθ2)).g_{\theta}^{\mathrm{FR}}(d\theta_{1},d\theta_{2}) = g_{p_{\theta}}^{\mathrm{FR}} \bigl( dp_{\theta}(d\theta_{1}), dp_{\theta}(d\theta_{2}) \bigr). In coordinates, GijFR(θ)=∫E∂θip(θ,x)∂θjp(θ,x)p(θ,x)dx=𝔼pθ[∂θilog⁡pθ∂θjlog⁡pθ],G_{ij}^{\mathrm{FR}}(\theta) = \int_{E} \frac{\partial_{\theta_{i}}p(\theta,x)\, \partial_{\theta_{j}}p(\theta,x)} {p(\theta,x)}\,dx = \mathbb{E}_{p_{\theta}} \Bigl[ \partial_{\theta_{i}}\log p_{\theta}\, \partial_{\theta_{j}}\log p_{\theta} \Bigr], which is the classical Fisher information matrix of Fisher [2] and Rao [4]. The matrix GFR(θ)G^{\mathrm{FR}}(\theta) defines a Riemannian metric on Θ\Theta as soon as it is smooth and positive definite, that is, as soon as dpθdp_{\theta} is injective.

4.1.2 Analysis of the metric

For u∈Tp𝒫+(E)u\in T_{p}\mathcal{P}_{+}(E), ∥u∥p,FR2=gpFR(u,u)=∫Eu(x)2p(x)dx=𝔼x∼p[(u(x)p(x))2].\|u\|_{p,\mathrm{FR}}^{2} = g_{p}^{\mathrm{FR}}(u,u) = \int_{E}\frac{u(x)^{2}}{p(x)}\,dx = \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p} \!\left[ \left(\frac{u(x)}{p(x)}\right)^{2} \right]. What enters the cost is the relative variation u/pu/p, not the absolute size of uu. The same density increment is expensive where pp is small and cheap where pp is concentrated. The left column of Figure 1 makes this visible on a Gaussian: an identical mass-preserving dipole costs far more in the tail than near the mode.

The metric is also invariant under reparametrization of the observation space. If ϕ:E→E′\phi:E\to E' is a diffeomorphism and densities are transported by ϕ\phi, the inner products gFRg^{\mathrm{FR}} are unchanged. Only the way densities differ from one another matters, not the coordinates chosen on EE. This invariance characterizes the Fisher–Rao metric in several infinite-dimensional settings; see Bauer, Bruveris, and Michor [3].

On the parameter space, the same geometry is intrinsic in the usual Riemannian sense: under a change of coordinates θ↦θ̃\theta\mapsto\widetilde\theta, the matrix GFRG^{\mathrm{FR}} transforms as a covariant 22-tensor. Distances and geodesics on (Θ,gFR)(\Theta,g^{\mathrm{FR}}) therefore do not depend on the chosen parametrization of the family.

4.2 Wasserstein-Otto

4.2.1 Kantorovich formulation of quadratic optimal transport

We begin directly from the Kantorovich formulation of optimal transport [7]. The general theory can be developed under substantially weaker assumptions, for instance for Borel probability measures on suitable metric spaces and for fairly general cost functions. Here we deliberately remain in the setting already used throughout this paper: EE is a finite-dimensional real vector space equipped with an inner product, and the probability measures under consideration admit strictly positive densities with respect to Lebesgue measure.

Because the quadratic transport cost involves the distance travelled by the mass, we restrict, when necessary, to densities with finite second moment and write 𝒫2,+(E)={p∈𝒫+(E):∫E∥x∥2p(x)dx<∞}.\mathcal P_{2,+}(E) = \left\{ p\in\mathcal P_+(E): \int_E \|x\|^2 p(x)\,dx < \infty \right\}. Let p,q∈𝒫2,+(E)p,q\in\mathcal P_{2,+}(E), and denote the corresponding probability measures by dμ(x)=p(x)dx,dν(y)=q(y)dy.d\mu(x)=p(x)\,dx, \qquad d\nu(y)=q(y)\,dy. A transport plan, or coupling, between pp and qq is a probability measure π\pi on E×EE\times E whose first and second marginals are μ\mu and ν\nu. Equivalently, if pr1(x,y)=x,pr2(x,y)=y,\mathrm{pr}_1(x,y)=x, \qquad \mathrm{pr}_2(x,y)=y, then (pr1)#π=μ,(pr2)#π=ν.(\mathrm{pr}_1)_\#\pi=\mu, \qquad (\mathrm{pr}_2)_\#\pi=\nu. We denote the set of such couplings by Π(p,q)={π∈𝒫(E×E):(pr1)#π=p(x)dx,(pr2)#π=q(y)dy}.\Pi(p,q) = \left\{ \pi\in\mathcal P(E\times E): (\mathrm{pr}_1)_\#\pi=p(x)\,dx,\; (\mathrm{pr}_2)_\#\pi=q(y)\,dy \right\}.

The quadratic Wasserstein distance is then defined by the Kantorovich minimization problem W22(p,q)=infπ∈Π(p,q)∫E×E∥x−y∥2dπ(x,y).\boxed{ W_2^2(p,q) = \inf_{\pi\in\Pi(p,q)} \int_{E\times E} \|x-y\|^2\,d\pi(x,y). } The interpretation is direct. The quantity dπ(x,y)d\pi(x,y) describes how much probability mass is coupled between a position xx in the first density and a position yy in the second one, while ∥x−y∥2\|x-y\|^2 measures the cost of moving that mass through the geometry of EE. The marginal constraints ensure that all the mass initially distributed according to pp is used and that the final distribution is exactly qq. Figure 5 illustrates this structure on ℝ\mathbb{R}: the joint density of a coupling occupies the product space, its marginals recover pp and qq, and the quadratic cost vanishes on the diagonal y=xy=x.

In the present finite-dimensional setting, and for p,qp,q with finite second moments, this gives a finite distance and the infimum is attained under the standard hypotheses. We shall not need here the more general measure-theoretic formulation of the Kantorovich problem. What matters for the comparison with Fisher–Rao is that the Wasserstein cost explicitly depends on the geometry of the underlying space through the factor ∥x−y∥2\|x-y\|^2. In particular, changing the spatial separation between two pieces of mass changes the transport cost even if the corresponding variations of probability density have the same local amplitude.

The static formula above compares two complete probability distributions. To obtain the infinitesimal Riemannian structure used by Otto, we next describe a differentiable path of densities t↦ptt\mapsto p_t through the velocity field that transports its mass. This leads to the continuity equation and, after minimizing the kinetic energy over all velocity fields producing the same tangent variation, to the Wasserstein–Otto metric.

4.2.2 Construction of the Wasserstein–Otto metric

The Kantorovich formulation is static: it assigns a transport cost between two complete probability distributions. To obtain a Riemannian metric, we now examine the same geometry at the infinitesimal scale. If a density pp changes with instantaneous variation uu, we ask how much spatial motion of the probability mass is required to produce that variation.

On the moving density and conservation of mass.

Let t↦ptt\mapsto p_t be a differentiable path of strictly positive probability densities, with p0=pp_0=p. We describe the motion of the mass by a time-dependent velocity field vtv_t and its flow ddtΦt(x)=vt(Φt(x)),Φ0(x)=x.\frac{d}{dt}\Phi_t(x) = v_t\bigl(\Phi_t(x)\bigr), \qquad \Phi_0(x)=x. At t=0t=0, the tangent variation u:=∂tpt|t=0u := \left.\partial_t p_t\right|_{t=0} is therefore represented by the instantaneous velocity field v=v0v=v_0 through u=−div⁡(pv).\boxed{ u = -\operatorname{div}(pv). } Integrating over EE, under the usual decay or boundary assumptions, gives ∫Eudx=0,\int_E u\,dx=0, which is exactly the first-order conservation of total probability mass.

The continuity equation is also the constraint appearing in the dynamic formulation of quadratic optimal transport. More precisely, the Benamou–Brenier formulation [8] writes W22(p0,p1)=inf(pt,vt)∫01∫Ept(x)∥vt(x)∥2dxdt,W_2^2(p_0,p_1) = \inf_{(p_t,v_t)} \int_0^1 \int_E p_t(x)\|v_t(x)\|^2 \,dx\,dt, where the infimum is taken over sufficiently regular paths satisfying ∂tpt+div⁡(ptvt)=0,pt=0=p0,pt=1=p1.\partial_t p_t + \operatorname{div}(p_t v_t) = 0, \qquad p_{t=0}=p_0, \qquad p_{t=1}=p_1. The kinetic quantity below is thus the infinitesimal energy density underlying the quadratic Wasserstein distance.

On the infinitesimal transport cost.

The quadratic Kantorovich cost measures squared spatial displacement. Along the flow, Φε(x)=x+εv(x)+o(ε),\Phi_\varepsilon(x) = x+\varepsilon v(x)+o(\varepsilon), so ∥Φε(x)−x∥2=ε2∥v(x)∥2+o(ε2).\|\Phi_\varepsilon(x)-x\|^2 = \varepsilon^2\|v(x)\|^2 + o(\varepsilon^2). This suggests the weighted kinetic norm ∥v∥p2=∫E∥v(x)∥2p(x)dx.\|v\|_p^2 = \int_E \|v(x)\|^2p(x)\,dx.

The tangent vector, however, is the density variation uu, not the velocity field vv. Moreover, the relation u=−div⁡(pv)u=-\operatorname{div}(pv) does not determine vv uniquely. Different particle motions may produce the same first-order variation of the density.

The Wasserstein–Otto norm is therefore defined by the least kinetic energy among all velocity fields that realize the same tangent variation: ∥u∥p,WO2:=infv−div⁡(pv)=u∫E∥v(x)∥2p(x)dx.\boxed{ \|u\|_{p,\mathrm{WO}}^2 := \inf_{\substack{v\\ -\operatorname{div}(pv)=u}} \int_E \|v(x)\|^2p(x)\,dx. } Figure 7 illustrates this cost for a mass-preserving variation uu: the minimizing velocity field v=∇ϕv=\nabla\phi moves the density, and the integrand ∥v∥2p\|v\|^2p is the local kinetic contribution.

Formally, the admissible tangent variations are thus TpWO𝒫+(E)={u:∫Eudx=0,u=−div(pv) for some v∈L2(E,pdx;E)}.T_p^{\mathrm{WO}}\mathcal P_+(E) = \left\{ u: \int_E u\,dx=0, \quad u=-\operatorname{div}(pv) \text{ for some } v\in L^2(E,p\,dx;E) \right\}.

At this stage, the geometry is already visible. A tangent variation uu records only the net first-order redistribution of probability mass. It does not remember all particle trajectories that may realize it. The Wasserstein–Otto norm retains, among all such realizations, the one with the smallest kinetic energy.

The constraint does not determine the velocity field uniquely. The next subsection identifies geometrically the part of a velocity field that is relevant to the density variation and shows that the minimizer is necessarily a gradient field.

4.2.3 Weighted Helmholtz decomposition and the minimal velocity field

The continuity equation does not determine a unique velocity field. Indeed, if vv realizes the tangent variation uu, u=−div⁡(pv),u=-\operatorname{div}(pv), then v+wv+w realizes the same variation whenever div⁡(pw)=0.\operatorname{div}(pw)=0. Such a field ww may move points of the underlying space while remaining invisible at the level of the density, at least to first order. Figure 8 shows a concrete example: for the isotropic Gaussian p=𝒩(0,I2)p=\mathcal N(0,I_2) and the rotational field v(y)=(−y2,y1)v(y)=(-y_2,y_1), one has div⁡v=0\operatorname{div}v=0 and ⟨∇p,v⟩=0\langle\nabla p,v\rangle=0, hence div⁡(pv)=0\operatorname{div}(pv)=0. The points circulate along the level sets of pp, yet the density itself does not change. The Wasserstein–Otto construction selects, among all velocity fields producing the same density variation, the one with minimal kinetic energy.

To make this geometric, fix a strictly positive density pp and consider the weighted Hilbert space ℋp=L2(E,p(x)dx;E),\mathcal H_p = L^2(E,p(x)\,dx;E), with inner product ⟨v1,v2⟩p=∫Ep(x)⟨v1(x),v2(x)⟩dx.\langle v_1,v_2\rangle_p = \int_E p(x)\, \langle v_1(x),v_2(x)\rangle \,dx. We assume throughout this discussion sufficient regularity and either sufficient decay at infinity or boundary conditions eliminating boundary terms.

Let 𝒢p={∇φ:φ∈Cc∞(E)}¯L2(p)\mathcal G_p = \overline{ \bigl\{ \nabla\varphi: \varphi\in C^\infty_c(E) \bigr\} }^{\,L^2(p)} denote the closed subspace generated by gradient fields. Its orthogonal complement has a simple interpretation. A vector field w∈ℋpw\in\mathcal H_p belongs to 𝒢p⟂\mathcal G_p^\perp precisely when ∫Ep⟨w,∇ψ⟩dx=0for every admissible ψ.\int_E p\,\langle w,\nabla\psi\rangle\,dx =0 \qquad \text{for every admissible }\psi. By integration by parts, ∫Ep⟨w,∇ψ⟩dx=−∫Eψdiv⁡(pw)dx.\int_E p\,\langle w,\nabla\psi\rangle\,dx = -\int_E \psi\,\operatorname{div}(pw)\,dx. Hence, in the weak sense, 𝒢p⟂=𝒦p,𝒦p:={w∈ℋp:div⁡(pw)=0}.\mathcal G_p^\perp = \mathcal K_p, \qquad \mathcal K_p := \bigl\{ w\in\mathcal H_p: \operatorname{div}(pw)=0 \bigr\}. Under the usual regularity assumptions, the projection onto 𝒢p\mathcal G_p is represented by a gradient. Thus any admissible velocity field may be written v=∇ϕ+w,div⁡(pw)=0,⟨∇ϕ,w⟩p=0.\boxed{ v = \nabla\phi + w, \qquad \operatorname{div}(pw)=0, \qquad \langle \nabla\phi,w\rangle_p=0. } The two components have very different meanings. The gradient part changes the density, while the weighted divergence-free part is invisible to the continuity equation: −div⁡(pv)=−div⁡(p∇ϕ).-\operatorname{div}(pv) = -\operatorname{div}(p\nabla\phi). Consequently, if vv realizes a prescribed tangent vector uu, then ∇ϕ\nabla\phi realizes the same uu. Orthogonality gives the Pythagorean identity ∥v∥p2=∥∇ϕ∥p2+∥w∥p2.\|v\|_p^2 = \|\nabla\phi\|_p^2 + \|w\|_p^2. The additional component ww can therefore only increase the kinetic energy without changing the induced density variation. The minimizing representative is obtained by setting w=0w=0: vmin=∇ϕu,v_{\min} = \nabla\phi_u, where ϕu\phi_u solves the weighted Poisson equation −div⁡(p∇ϕu)=u.\boxed{ -\operatorname{div} \bigl(p\nabla\phi_u\bigr) = u. } It follows that ∥u∥p,WO2=infv:−div⁡(pv)=u∫Ep∥v∥2dx=∫Ep∥∇ϕu∥2dx.\boxed{ \|u\|_{p,\mathrm{WO}}^2 = \inf_{v:\,-\operatorname{div}(pv)=u} \int_E p\|v\|^2\,dx = \int_E p\|\nabla\phi_u\|^2\,dx. } Figure 9 shows the same decomposition in coordinates: a general velocity field splits into a gradient part that changes the density and a weighted divergence-free part that does not.

This also gives a quotient interpretation. At a fixed density pp, two velocity fields represent the same tangent variation whenever they differ by an element of 𝒦p\mathcal K_p. Formally, Tp𝒫+(E)≃ℋp/𝒦p,T_p\mathcal P_+(E) \simeq \mathcal H_p/\mathcal K_p, and the gradient field ∇ϕu\nabla\phi_u is the canonical representative of minimal L2(p)L^2(p)-norm in the corresponding equivalence class. In this sense, the Wasserstein tangent geometry keeps the part of a velocity field that actually transports the density and discards weighted divergence-free circulations.

4.2.3.1 A variational reading.

The same conclusion follows directly from the constrained minimization problem. Introduce a Lagrange multiplier ϕ\phi and consider ℒ(v,ϕ)=12∫Ep∥v∥2dx−∫Eϕ(−div⁡(pv)−u)dx.\mathcal L(v,\phi) = \frac12 \int_E p\|v\|^2\,dx - \int_E \phi\, \bigl( -\operatorname{div}(pv)-u \bigr) \,dx. After integration by parts, ℒ(v,ϕ)=12∫Ep∥v∥2dx−∫Ep⟨∇ϕ,v⟩dx+∫Eϕudx.\mathcal L(v,\phi) = \frac12 \int_E p\|v\|^2\,dx - \int_E p\,\langle\nabla\phi,v\rangle\,dx + \int_E \phi u\,dx. Stationarity with respect to vv gives v=∇ϕ,v=\nabla\phi, and the constraint then yields −div⁡(p∇ϕ)=u.-\operatorname{div}(p\nabla\phi)=u. Thus the Euler–Lagrange condition and the weighted Helmholtz decomposition express the same geometry: the Wasserstein–Otto norm is obtained by orthogonally projecting an arbitrary admissible velocity field onto the gradient subspace.

4.2.3.2 The Wasserstein–Otto metric.

We can now complete the construction. For tangent variations u,wu,w, let ϕu\phi_u and ϕw\phi_w be the potentials of their minimal velocity fields, that is, −div⁡(p∇ϕu)=u,−div⁡(p∇ϕw)=w.-\operatorname{div}(p\nabla\phi_u)=u, \qquad -\operatorname{div}(p\nabla\phi_w)=w. The Wasserstein–Otto inner product is defined by the weighted L2(p)L^2(p) inner product of these minimal representatives: gpWO(u,w)=∫Ep(x)⟨∇ϕu(x),∇ϕw(x)⟩dx.\boxed{ g_p^{\mathrm{WO}}(u,w) = \int_E p(x) \left\langle \nabla\phi_u(x), \nabla\phi_w(x) \right\rangle dx. } In particular, gpWO(u,u)=∥u∥p,WO2=∫Ep(x)∥∇ϕu(x)∥2dx.g_p^{\mathrm{WO}}(u,u) = \|u\|_{p,\mathrm{WO}}^2 = \int_E p(x)\|\nabla\phi_u(x)\|^2\,dx. This is the formal Riemannian metric associated with the quadratic Wasserstein distance [5], [9].

4.2.3.3 Pullback to a parametric family.

Let p:Θ→𝒫+(E),θ↦pθ,p:\Theta\longrightarrow\mathcal P_+(E), \qquad \theta\longmapsto p_\theta, be a smooth finite-dimensional family. A parameter displacement dθ∈TθΘd\theta\in T_\theta\Theta induces the density variation udθ=dpθ(dθ).u_{d\theta} = dp_\theta(d\theta). Then the matrix of the pullback metric is GijWO(θ)=∫Epθ(x)⟨∇ϕi(x),∇ϕj(x)⟩dx.\boxed{ G_{ij}^{\mathrm{WO}}(\theta) = \int_E p_\theta(x) \left\langle \nabla\phi_i(x), \nabla\phi_j(x) \right\rangle dx. } This is the expression that will be used throughout the Atlas. The construction is therefore complete: a parameter variation first produces a density variation, this variation determines a minimal transport field through a weighted elliptic equation, and the metric measures the weighted kinetic pairing of the resulting fields. This parametric Wasserstein construction is developed by Chen and Li [12] (Section 3.1).

4.2.4 Analysis of the metric

The construction above already reveals the main geometric feature of the Wasserstein–Otto metric. Fisher–Rao measures a density variation directly, whereas Wasserstein–Otto measures the spatial motion required to realize that variation. The geometry of the observation space EE therefore enters the metric explicitly.

4.2.4.1 A nonlocal metric.

For Fisher–Rao, the cost of a tangent variation is read pointwise from u/pu/p. For Wasserstein–Otto, the variation uu first has to be converted into its minimal velocity field by solving −div⁡(p∇ϕu)=u.-\operatorname{div}(p\nabla\phi_u)=u. The value of ∇ϕu\nabla\phi_u at one point generally depends on the variation of the density over the whole domain. The metric is therefore nonlocal: a local change in uu can modify the transport field away from the point where the change was introduced.

Equivalently, introducing formally the weighted elliptic operator ℒpϕ=−div⁡(p∇ϕ),\mathcal L_p\phi = -\operatorname{div}(p\nabla\phi), one may write, on zero-mass variations and modulo additive constants, ϕu=ℒp−1u.\phi_u=\mathcal L_p^{-1}u. This gives the formal identity gpWO(u,w)=∫Eϕu(x)w(x)dx=∫Eu(x)ϕw(x)dx,g_p^{\mathrm{WO}}(u,w) = \int_E \phi_u(x)w(x)\,dx = \int_E u(x)\phi_w(x)\,dx, after integration by parts. In this sense, the Wasserstein–Otto norm has the character of a weighted negative-order Sobolev norm [5], [9].

4.2.4.2 Dependence on the geometry of EE.

The metric depends on the inner product of the observation space through the kinetic quantity ∫Ep(x)∥∇ϕu(x)∥2dx.\int_E p(x)\|\nabla\phi_u(x)\|^2\,dx. This makes Wasserstein geometry sensitive to spatial scale and spatial distance.

For example, if T(x)=Qx+b,T(x)=Qx+b, with QQ orthogonal, then TT is an isometry of EE. Transporting both densities by TT leaves the quadratic transport cost unchanged, and the same invariance is inherited infinitesimally by the Wasserstein–Otto metric.

A general diffeomorphism of the observation space does not have this property if the original metric on EE is kept fixed. A dilation already shows the difference. If Sa(x)=ax,a≠0,S_a(x)=ax, \qquad a\neq0, then W2((Sa)#p,(Sa)#q)=|a|W2(p,q),W_2\bigl((S_a)_\#p,(S_a)_\#q\bigr) = |a|\,W_2(p,q), so the squared infinitesimal cost scales by a2a^2. The physical scale of the observation space is therefore part of the Wasserstein geometry.

This contrasts sharply with Fisher–Rao, whose metric is invariant under smooth reparametrizations of the observation space. The distinction is not a failure of coordinate invariance on the parameter manifold Θ\Theta: once either metric has been pulled back to Θ\Theta, its matrix transforms as a covariant 22-tensor under a change of parameter coordinates. The difference lies in what the ambient metric regards as geometrically meaningful before the pullback.

4.2.4.3 Translation as the elementary transport direction.

The simplest example is a pure translation. Let a∈Ea\in E and consider pt(x)=p(x−ta).p_t(x)=p(x-ta). Then u(x)=∂tpt(x)|t=0=−⟨a,∇p(x)⟩=−div⁡(p(x)a).u(x) = \left.\partial_t p_t(x)\right|_{t=0} = -\langle a,\nabla p(x)\rangle = -\operatorname{div}(p(x)a). The constant field v(x)=av(x)=a realizes this variation. Since a=∇⟨a,x⟩,a=\nabla\langle a,x\rangle, it already belongs to the gradient subspace and is therefore the minimal representative. Hence ∥u∥p,WO2=∫Ep(x)∥a∥2dx=∥a∥2.\|u\|_{p,\mathrm{WO}}^2 = \int_E p(x)\|a\|^2\,dx = \|a\|^2. The infinitesimal cost of translating a probability distribution is thus exactly the squared Euclidean speed of the translation, independently of the shape of the density.

This elementary case already anticipates a recurrent feature of the Atlas. For location parameters, Wasserstein–Otto often retains the Euclidean geometry of the underlying space, while Fisher–Rao weights the same parameter displacement according to the relative change it induces in the density.

4.3 Recapitulation

We now summarize the two ambient geometries before applying them to the parametric families of the Atlas. Let p∈𝒫+(E)p\in\mathcal P_+(E) and let uu be an admissible tangent variation, so that ∫Eu(x)dx=0.\int_E u(x)\,dx=0.

For Fisher–Rao, the squared norm is ∥u∥p,FR2=∫Eu(x)2p(x)dx.\boxed{ \|u\|_{p,\mathrm{FR}}^2 = \int_E \frac{u(x)^2}{p(x)} \,dx. } It acts directly on the density variation. Equivalently, ∥u∥p,FR2=𝔼x∼p[(u(x)p(x))2],\|u\|_{p,\mathrm{FR}}^2 = \mathbb E_{x\sim p} \left[ \left( \frac{u(x)}{p(x)} \right)^2 \right], so Fisher–Rao measures the size of the relative statistical variation u/pu/p.

For Wasserstein–Otto, the same density variation is represented through a velocity field. Among all vv satisfying u=−div⁡(pv),u=-\operatorname{div}(pv), the metric selects the field of minimal kinetic energy. The weighted Helmholtz decomposition shows that this minimizer is a gradient field, vu=∇ϕu,\boxed{ v_u=\nabla\phi_u, } where the potential ϕu\phi_u, defined up to an additive constant, solves −div⁡(p∇ϕu)=u.\boxed{ -\operatorname{div} \bigl( p\nabla\phi_u \bigr) = u. } Hence ∥u∥p,WO2=∫Ep(x)∥∇ϕu(x)∥2dx.\boxed{ \|u\|_{p,\mathrm{WO}}^2 = \int_E p(x)\|\nabla\phi_u(x)\|^2 \,dx. } Equivalently, ∥u∥p,WO2=infv−div⁡(pv)=u∫Ep(x)∥v(x)∥2dx.\|u\|_{p,\mathrm{WO}}^2 = \inf_{\substack{v\\-\operatorname{div}(pv)=u}} \int_E p(x)\|v(x)\|^2 \,dx. The two metrics therefore assign a very different meaning to the same tangent variation. Figure 11 shows this on a one-dimensional example: the same mass-preserving uu is read either through the relative integrand u2/pu^{2}/p or through the kinetic density ∥v∥2p\|v\|^{2}p of the minimal transport field.

4.3.0.1 Fisher–Rao: statistical variation without spatial geometry.

Fisher–Rao compares how probability density changes relative to its current value. Its cost is pointwise: the same absolute variation is more expensive where pp is small and less expensive where pp is large. More fundamentally, Fisher–Rao does not require a notion of distance between points of the observation space. If the observation space is smoothly reparameterized and the densities are pushed forward accordingly, the metric is unchanged. In this sense, Fisher–Rao is agnostic to the particular coordinate system used on EE; its geometry is attached to the statistical variation itself rather than to distances in the sample space. This diffeomorphism invariance is a characteristic property of the Fisher–Rao metric in broad classes of smooth density manifolds [3].

4.3.0.2 Wasserstein–Otto: transport through the geometry of EE.

Wasserstein–Otto instead asks how probability mass must move in order to produce the variation uu. The norm depends explicitly on the inner product of EE through the kinetic energy ∫Ep∥∇ϕu∥2dx,\int_E p\|\nabla\phi_u\|^2\,dx, and the potential ϕu\phi_u is obtained by solving a weighted elliptic equation on EE. The metric is therefore sensitive to spatial distance, scale, and direction. Euclidean isometries preserve the transport geometry, whereas a general deformation of the observation space changes the metric unless the geometry of EE is transformed with it [5], [9].

5 The one-dimensional bridge

On the real line, the continuity equation can be integrated explicitly. This gives a direct expression for the Wasserstein–Otto metric in terms of primitives of density variations, and then in terms of quantiles. Recall that 𝒫2,+(E)={p∈𝒫+(E):∫E∥x∥2p(x)dx<∞}\mathcal P_{2,+}(E) = \left\{ p\in\mathcal P_+(E): \int_E \|x\|^2p(x)\,dx<\infty \right\} is the space of strictly positive probability densities with finite second moment. In this section, E=ℝE=\mathbb{R}, so the moment condition is ∫ℝx2p(x)dx<∞\int_\mathbb{R}x^2p(x)\,dx<\infty.

5.1 From a density variation to a flux

Let p∈𝒫2,+(ℝ)p\in\mathcal P_{2,+}(\mathbb{R}), and let u,wu,w be integrable density variations with zero total mass. Define their primitives by U(x)=∫−∞xu(s)ds,W(x)=∫−∞xw(s)ds.U(x)=\int_{-\infty}^x u(s)\,ds, \qquad W(x)=\int_{-\infty}^x w(s)\,ds. 5.3.3 Wasserstein–Otto pullback

Let FF and QQ denote the cumulative distribution function and quantile function of the reference density pp. Direct integration gives Fμ,σ(x)=F(x−μσ).F_{\mu,\sigma}(x) =F\!\left(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right). 5.3.4 Geometry and comparison

5.3.4.1 Wasserstein–Otto: a constant metric and straight parameter paths.

The coefficients of dsWO2ds_{\mathrm{WO}}^2 do not depend on μ\mu or σ\sigma. The parameter half-plane therefore has the Euclidean metric directly in (μ,σ)(\mu,\sigma) coordinates. Location and scale are orthogonal, and the Gaussian curvature is zero.

For endpoints (μ0,σ0)(\mu_0,\sigma_0) and (μ1,σ1)(\mu_1,\sigma_1), the path μ(t)=(1−t)μ0+tμ1,σ(t)=(1−t)σ0+tσ1,0≤t≤1,\mu(t)=(1-t)\mu_0+t\mu_1, \qquad \sigma(t)=(1-t)\sigma_0+t\sigma_1, \qquad 0\leq t\leq1, remains in the parameter domain because both endpoint scales are positive. Moreover, Qμ(t),σ(t)(r)=μ(t)+σ(t)Q(r)=(1−t)Qμ0,σ0(r)+tQμ1,σ1(r).\begin{aligned} Q_{\mu(t),\sigma(t)}(r) &=\mu(t)+\sigma(t)Q(r)\\ &=(1-t)Q_{\mu_0,\sigma_0}(r)+tQ_{\mu_1,\sigma_1}(r). \end{aligned} This verifies, for this specific family, the condition established in the previous subsection: the ambient straight quantile segment is realized by a parameter path. The path is consequently a constant-speed minimizing geodesic for the pullback metric, and its intrinsic distance equals the ambient Wasserstein distance: dgWO((μ0,σ0),(μ1,σ1))2=W2(pμ0,σ0,pμ1,σ1)2=(μ1−μ0)2+(σ1−σ0)2.\begin{aligned} &d_{g^{\mathrm{WO}}}\bigl((\mu_0,\sigma_0),(\mu_1,\sigma_1)\bigr)^2 =W_2(p_{\mu_0,\sigma_0},p_{\mu_1,\sigma_1})^2\\ &\quad=(\mu_1-\mu_0)^2+(\sigma_1-\sigma_0)^2. \end{aligned} The boundary σ=0\sigma=0 is at finite transport distance. Indeed, at fixed μ\mu, the length required to decrease the scale from σ0\sigma_0 to zero is σ0\sigma_0. The limiting distribution is the point mass at μ\mu, outside our space of strictly positive densities. The open parameter half-plane is therefore not complete for this metric. Figure 16 shows how different the Fisher–Rao and Wasserstein–Otto geodesics already are between the same two Gaussian endpoints: the Wasserstein–Otto path translates at fixed variance, while the Fisher–Rao path broadens and then narrows again.

5.3.4.2 Fisher–Rao: a hyperbolic metric.

The factor 1/σ21/\sigma^2 changes the geometry. At a smaller scale, the same parameter displacement has a larger Fisher–Rao norm. Completing the square gives dsFR2=Apσ2(dμ+BpApdσ)2+Cp−Bp2/Apσ2dσ2.ds_{\mathrm{FR}}^2 =\frac{A_p}{\sigma^2} \left(d\mu+\frac{B_p}{A_p}d\sigma\right)^2 +\frac{C_p-B_p^2/A_p}{\sigma^2}\,d\sigma^2. Under the linear change of half-plane coordinates (μ,σ)↦(μ+BpApσ,ApCp−Bp2Apσ),(\mu,\sigma)\longmapsto \left(\mu+\frac{B_p}{A_p}\sigma, \frac{\sqrt{A_p C_p-B_p^2}}{A_p}\sigma\right), this is the standard hyperbolic half-plane metric multiplied by Cp−Bp2/ApC_p-B_p^2/A_p. Its Gaussian curvature is therefore the negative constant KFR=−1Cp−Bp2/Ap,KWO=0.\boxed{K_{\mathrm{FR}}=-\frac{1}{C_p-B_p^2/A_p}, \qquad K_{\mathrm{WO}}=0.} In the displayed half-plane coordinates, Fisher–Rao geodesics are vertical lines or semicircles orthogonal to the boundary. They are geodesics of the pullback metric on this family; no claim is made that they are ambient Fisher–Rao geodesics in the full density space.

For an even reference density, Bp=0B_p=0, and their traces in the original parameter coordinates are vertical lines μ=constant\mu=\text{constant} or the upper halves of ellipses ApCp(μ−μc)2+σ2=R2,\frac{A_p}{C_p}(\mu-\mu_c)^2+\sigma^2=R^2, where μc∈ℝ\mu_c\in\mathbb{R} and R>0R>0 specify the geodesic. Unlike the Wasserstein case, the boundary σ=0\sigma=0 is at infinite intrinsic distance: for every path, LgFR≥Cp−Bp2/Ap∫|σ̇(t)|σ(t)dt.L_{g^{\mathrm{FR}}} \geq\sqrt{C_p-B_p^2/A_p} \int\frac{|\dot\sigma(t)|}{\sigma(t)}\,dt. The hyperbolic half-plane description also shows that the Fisher–Rao pullback is complete. For the hyperbolic description with a symmetric reference density, see [14] (Example 5).

5.3.4.3 Gaussian and Laplace examples.

For the standard Gaussian reference density, p(x)=12πe−x2/2,p′(x)=−xp(x),p(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-x^2/2}, \qquad p'(x)=-x p(x), the Gaussian moments give Ap=1A_p=1, Bp=0B_p=0, and Cp=2C_p=2. Their Wasserstein–Otto metrics coincide, while their Fisher–Rao metrics and curvatures differ. This comparison isolates the distinction: Wasserstein–Otto uses only the first two reference moments, fixed here by standardization, whereas Fisher–Rao retains information about the shape of the density through p′p' and assigns a scale-dependent cost to parameter displacements.

6 Multivariate Gaussian family

We now consider Gaussian densities on ℝn\mathbb{R}^n. The mean is a vector μ∈ℝn\mu\in\mathbb{R}^n, and the covariance is a symmetric positive-definite matrix Σ∈Sn++(ℝ)\Sigma\in S_n^{++}(\mathbb{R}). The parameter space is therefore Θ=ℝn×Sn++(ℝ),\Theta=\mathbb{R}^n\times S_n^{++}(\mathbb{R}), and the family is given by pμ,Σ(x)=1(2π)n/2det⁡(Σ)1/2exp⁡(−12(x−μ)⊤Σ−1(x−μ)),x∈ℝn.p_{\mu,\Sigma}(x) =\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}\det(\Sigma)^{1/2}} \exp\!\left(-\frac12 (x-\mu)^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu)\right), \qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^n. Each density is strictly positive and has finite second moment, so (μ,Σ)↦pμ,Σ(\mu,\Sigma)\mapsto p_{\mu,\Sigma} takes values in 𝒫2,+(ℝn)\mathcal P_{2,+}(\mathbb{R}^n). The mean determines its position, while the covariance determines its dispersion and orientation. For n=1n=1, Σ=σ2\Sigma=\sigma^2 recovers the Gaussian location–dispersion family of Section 5.3.

6.1 Tangent directions and density differential

Let Sn(ℝ)S_n(\mathbb{R}) denote the vector space of real symmetric n×nn\times n matrices. Since Sn++(ℝ)S_n^{++}(\mathbb{R}) is an open subset of Sn(ℝ)S_n(\mathbb{R}), Tμℝn=ℝn,TΣSn++(ℝ)=Sn(ℝ).T_\mu\mathbb{R}^n=\mathbb{R}^n, \qquad T_\Sigma S_n^{++}(\mathbb{R})=S_n(\mathbb{R}). The matrix dΣd\Sigma need not be positive definite: it describes a variation of the covariance, whereas Σ\Sigma itself must remain positive definite. For every such direction, Σ+tdΣ\Sigma+t\,d\Sigma remains positive definite for sufficiently small |t||t|.

We recall the differential briefly. For a detailed computation, see Pierre Guilbert’s Pullback Fisher–Rao Geometries of Parametric Probability Families: An Expository Atlas, Section 4.2, “Multivariate Gaussian”, paragraph “Differential” [1]. 6.2 Wasserstein–Otto metric

For a tangent direction (dμ,dΣ)∈T(μ,Σ)Θ=ℝn×Sn(ℝ)(d\mu,d\Sigma)\in T_{(\mu,\Sigma)}\Theta =\mathbb{R}^n\times S_n(\mathbb{R}), the Wasserstein–Otto construction requires a potential whose gradient transports the density with the prescribed variation. We therefore seek ϕ\phi satisfying −div⁡(pμ,Σ∇ϕ)=dpμ,Σ(dμ,dΣ).-\operatorname{div}\bigl(p_{\mu,\Sigma}\nabla\phi\bigr) =dp_{\mu,\Sigma}(d\mu,d\Sigma). For Gaussian densities, a quadratic potential suffices. This reduces the weighted Poisson equation to a linear equation for a symmetric matrix; see Malagò, Montrucchio, and Pistone [10] (Sections 1.2 and 4) for the Gaussian potential representation and its Lyapunov formulation.

6.2.1 The transport potential

Let A∈Sn(ℝ)A\in S_n(\mathbb{R}) be a symmetric matrix to be determined, and consider ϕ(x)=dμ⊤(x−μ)+12(x−μ)⊤A(x−μ).\phi(x)=d\mu^{\top}(x-\mu) +\frac12(x-\mu)^{\top}A(x-\mu). 6.2.2 The pullback metric

For two parameter directions (dμ1,dΣ1)(d\mu_1,d\Sigma_1) and (dμ2,dΣ2)(d\mu_2,d\Sigma_2), write A1=ℒΣ(dΣ1),A2=ℒΣ(dΣ2).A_1=\mathcal L_\Sigma(d\Sigma_1), \qquad A_2=\mathcal L_\Sigma(d\Sigma_2). 6.2.2.1 Reduction to the one-dimensional Gaussian family.

For n=1n=1, write Σ=σ2\Sigma=\sigma^2 and dΣ=2σdσd\Sigma=2\sigma\,d\sigma. The Lyapunov equation becomes 2σ2A=2σdσ,A=dσσ.2\sigma^2 A=2\sigma\,d\sigma, \qquad A=\frac{d\sigma}{\sigma}. Consequently, 12ℒσ2(dΣ)dΣ=12dσσ(2σdσ)=dσ2,\frac12\mathcal L_{\sigma^2}(d\Sigma)\,d\Sigma =\frac12\frac{d\sigma}{\sigma}(2\sigma\,d\sigma) =d\sigma^2, and the metric reduces to dsWO2=dμ2+dσ2ds_{\mathrm{WO}}^2=d\mu^2+d\sigma^2, in agreement with Section 5.3. This also distinguishes the covariance coordinate Σ\Sigma from the standard-deviation coordinate σ\sigma used on the line.

6.3 Fisher–Rao metric

We now evaluate the Fisher–Rao metric on the same parameter and tangent spaces. The computation uses the density differential from Section 6.1 and the second and fourth moments of a Gaussian distribution. For the detailed calculation, see P. Guilbert, Pullback Fisher–Rao Geometries of Parametric Probability Families: An Expository Atlas, Section 4.2, “Multivariate Gaussian”, under “Metric” [1]. For the classical Riemannian treatment of the multivariate normal model, see Skovgaard [16].

For (dμ1,dΣ1),(dμ2,dΣ2)∈ℝn×Sn(ℝ)(d\mu_1,d\Sigma_1),(d\mu_2,d\Sigma_2) \in\mathbb{R}^n\times S_n(\mathbb{R}), the pullback is gμ,ΣFR((dμ1,dΣ1),(dμ2,dΣ2))=∫ℝndpμ,Σ(dμ1,dΣ1)(x)dpμ,Σ(dμ2,dΣ2)(x)pμ,Σ(x)dx.\begin{aligned} &g_{\mu,\Sigma}^{\mathrm{FR}} \bigl((d\mu_1,d\Sigma_1),(d\mu_2,d\Sigma_2)\bigr)\\ &\quad=\int_{\mathbb{R}^n} \frac{dp_{\mu,\Sigma}(d\mu_1,d\Sigma_1)(x)\, dp_{\mu,\Sigma}(d\mu_2,d\Sigma_2)(x)} {p_{\mu,\Sigma}(x)}\,dx. \end{aligned} The logarithmic differential consists of a term linear in x−μx-\mu, associated with dμd\mu, and a centered quadratic term associated with dΣd\Sigma. We evaluate their products separately.

6.3.0.1 Mean directions.

The covariance identity is ∫ℝn(x−μ)(x−μ)⊤pμ,Σ(x)dx=Σ.\int_{\mathbb{R}^n}(x-\mu)(x-\mu)^{\top}p_{\mu,\Sigma}(x)\,dx=\Sigma. It gives ∫ℝn((x−μ)⊤Σ−1dμ1)((x−μ)⊤Σ−1dμ2)pμ,Σ(x)dx=dμ1⊤Σ−1ΣΣ−1dμ2=dμ1⊤Σ−1dμ2.\begin{aligned} &\int_{\mathbb{R}^n} \bigl((x-\mu)^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}d\mu_1\bigr) \bigl((x-\mu)^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}d\mu_2\bigr) p_{\mu,\Sigma}(x)\,dx\\ &\quad=d\mu_1^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}\Sigma\Sigma^{-1}d\mu_2 =d\mu_1^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}d\mu_2. \end{aligned}

6.3.0.2 Mixed directions.

The product of a mean contribution and a covariance contribution is odd in x−μx-\mu. Its integral against the centered Gaussian density is zero. Thus mean and covariance directions are orthogonal for Fisher–Rao, as for Wasserstein–Otto.

6.3.0.3 Covariance directions.

For X∼𝒩(μ,Σ)X\sim\mathcal N(\mu,\Sigma) and symmetric matrices B,CB,C, the Gaussian fourth-moment identity reads [17] Cov⁡((X−μ)⊤B(X−μ),(X−μ)⊤C(X−μ))=2tr⁡(BΣCΣ).\operatorname{Cov}\!\left( (X-\mu)^{\top}B(X-\mu), (X-\mu)^{\top}C(X-\mu)\right) =2\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(B\Sigma C\Sigma). In the density differential, the two matrices are B=Σ−1dΣ1Σ−1,C=Σ−1dΣ2Σ−1.B=\Sigma^{-1}d\Sigma_1\Sigma^{-1}, \qquad C=\Sigma^{-1}d\Sigma_2\Sigma^{-1}. For i=1,2i=1,2, the corresponding quadratic form has expectation tr⁡(Σ−1dΣi).\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\Sigma^{-1}d\Sigma_i). This is exactly the trace term subtracted in the differential. The covariance block is therefore one quarter of the covariance of these two quadratic forms. Applying the identity above gives gμ,ΣFR((0,dΣ1),(0,dΣ2))=12tr⁡((Σ−1dΣ1Σ−1)Σ(Σ−1dΣ2Σ−1)Σ)=12tr⁡(Σ−1dΣ1Σ−1dΣ2).\begin{aligned} &g_{\mu,\Sigma}^{\mathrm{FR}} \bigl((0,d\Sigma_1),(0,d\Sigma_2)\bigr)\\ &\quad=\frac12\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\!\left( (\Sigma^{-1}d\Sigma_1\Sigma^{-1})\Sigma (\Sigma^{-1}d\Sigma_2\Sigma^{-1})\Sigma\right)\\ &\quad=\frac12\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\Sigma^{-1}d\Sigma_1\Sigma^{-1}d\Sigma_2). \end{aligned} Combining the three blocks yields gμ,ΣFR((dμ1,dΣ1),(dμ2,dΣ2))=dμ1⊤Σ−1dμ2+12tr⁡(Σ−1dΣ1Σ−1dΣ2).\boxed{ \begin{aligned} &g_{\mu,\Sigma}^{\mathrm{FR}} \bigl((d\mu_1,d\Sigma_1),(d\mu_2,d\Sigma_2)\bigr)\\ &\quad=d\mu_1^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}d\mu_2 +\frac12\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\Sigma^{-1}d\Sigma_1\Sigma^{-1}d\Sigma_2). \end{aligned} } Equivalently, dsFR2=dμ⊤Σ−1dμ+12tr⁡(Σ−1dΣΣ−1dΣ).\boxed{ ds_{\mathrm{FR}}^2 =d\mu^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}d\mu +\frac12\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\Sigma^{-1}d\Sigma\,\Sigma^{-1}d\Sigma). } The covariance contribution can also be written as 12tr⁡((Σ−1/2dΣΣ−1/2)2).\frac12\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\!\left((\Sigma^{-1/2}d\Sigma\,\Sigma^{-1/2})^2\right). It is nonnegative and vanishes only when dΣ=0d\Sigma=0. Together with positive definiteness of Σ−1\Sigma^{-1}, this establishes positive definiteness of the pullback.

For mean variations, Fisher–Rao weights directions by Σ−1\Sigma^{-1}: smaller variance means greater cost. The transport metric instead gives the Euclidean squared norm ∥dμ∥2\|d\mu\|^2.

For covariance variations, Fisher–Rao uses Σ−1\Sigma^{-1} on both sides of dΣd\Sigma, while the transport metric uses the inverse Lyapunov operator. The mixed block is zero for both metrics. However, the mean block of Fisher–Rao depends on Σ\Sigma, so this orthogonality does not make it a product of independent mean and covariance metrics.

For n=1n=1, substituting Σ=σ2\Sigma=\sigma^2 and dΣ=2σdσd\Sigma=2\sigma\,d\sigma gives dsFR2=dμ2σ2+12(2σdσ)2σ4=dμ2+2dσ2σ2,ds_{\mathrm{FR}}^2 =\frac{d\mu^2}{\sigma^2} +\frac12\frac{(2\sigma\,d\sigma)^2}{\sigma^4} =\frac{d\mu^2+2\,d\sigma^2}{\sigma^2}, in agreement with the Gaussian example in Section 5.3.

6.4 Comparison: mean, principal scales, rotations, and geodesics

The two metrics act on the same tangent space T(μ,Σ)Θ=ℝn×Sn(ℝ)T_{(\mu,\Sigma)}\Theta=\mathbb{R}^n\times S_n(\mathbb{R}), but assign different costs to its mean and covariance components. We first compare their action on one tangent direction, then describe the resulting paths in parameter space.

6.4.1 Mean and covariance directions

For a pure mean variation, the squared norms are gμ,ΣFR((dμ,0),(dμ,0))=dμ⊤Σ−1dμ,gμ,ΣWO((dμ,0),(dμ,0))=∥dμ∥2.\boxed{ \begin{aligned} g_{\mu,\Sigma}^{\mathrm{FR}}\bigl((d\mu,0),(d\mu,0)\bigr) &=d\mu^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}d\mu,\\ g_{\mu,\Sigma}^{\mathrm{WO}}\bigl((d\mu,0),(d\mu,0)\bigr) &=\|d\mu\|^2. \end{aligned} } Fisher–Rao measures the displacement relative to the current dispersion: shifting a narrow distribution is more costly than shifting a broad one by the same amount. The transport cost of a translation is its squared Euclidean length, independently of the covariance.

For a pure covariance variation, the comparison is gμ,ΣFR((0,dΣ),(0,dΣ))=12tr⁡(Σ−1dΣΣ−1dΣ),gμ,ΣWO((0,dΣ),(0,dΣ))=12tr⁡(ℒΣ(dΣ)dΣ).\boxed{ \begin{aligned} g_{\mu,\Sigma}^{\mathrm{FR}}\bigl((0,d\Sigma),(0,d\Sigma)\bigr) &=\frac12\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\Sigma^{-1}d\Sigma\,\Sigma^{-1}d\Sigma),\\ g_{\mu,\Sigma}^{\mathrm{WO}}\bigl((0,d\Sigma),(0,d\Sigma)\bigr) &=\frac12\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\bigl(\mathcal L_\Sigma(d\Sigma)\,d\Sigma\bigr). \end{aligned} } The first expression compares the covariance change with the covariance itself. The second measures the kinetic energy of the affine gradient field producing that change. To make their respective weights explicit, we decompose the covariance into its principal scales and orientation.

6.4.2 Rotation–scale coordinates for the covariance

Write the spectral decomposition in terms of standard deviations along the principal axes: Σ=Rdiag⁡(σ12,…,σn2)R⊤,σi>0,R∈SO(n).\Sigma=R\,\mathop{\mathrm{diag}}(\sigma_1^2,\ldots,\sigma_n^2)R^{\top}, \qquad \sigma_i>0,\qquad R\in SO(n). Here RR is a rotation matrix whose columns are an oriented orthonormal basis of eigenvectors, and SO(n)SO(n) denotes the group of orthogonal matrices of determinant one. Locally, when the eigenvalues are distinct, we fix their order and choose the eigenvectors smoothly. The covariance is then described by the nn principal scales and the rotation RR. Figure 19 shows this for a three-dimensional Gaussian: the principal axes of the density ellipsoid are the columns of RR, and the semi-axis lengths are the σi\sigma_i.

A rotation direction dR∈TRSO(n)dR\in T_R SO(n) satisfies R⊤dR+(dR)⊤R=0R^{\top}dR+(dR)^{\top}R=0. Define its infinitesimal rotation matrix by Ω=R⊤dR,Ω⊤=−Ω,dR=RΩ.\Omega=R^{\top}dR, \qquad \Omega^{\top}=-\Omega, \qquad dR=R\Omega. Differentiating the spectral decomposition gives R⊤dΣR=diag⁡(2σ1dσ1,…,2σndσn)+Ωdiag⁡(σ12,…,σn2)−diag⁡(σ12,…,σn2)Ω.\begin{aligned} R^{\top}d\Sigma R &=\mathop{\mathrm{diag}}(2\sigma_1d\sigma_1,\ldots,2\sigma_nd\sigma_n)\\ &\quad+\Omega\mathop{\mathrm{diag}}(\sigma_1^2,\ldots,\sigma_n^2) -\mathop{\mathrm{diag}}(\sigma_1^2,\ldots,\sigma_n^2)\Omega. \end{aligned} In particular, (R⊤dΣR)ii=2σidσi,(R⊤dΣR)ij=(σj2−σi2)Ωij(i≠j).(R^{\top}d\Sigma R)_{ii}=2\sigma_i d\sigma_i, \qquad (R^{\top}d\Sigma R)_{ij}=(\sigma_j^2-\sigma_i^2)\Omega_{ij} \quad(i\neq j). The diagonal entries record scale changes; the off-diagonal entries record changes of orientation. These are entries in the current principal-axis basis, not new independent covariance parameters.

In this basis, multiplication by Σ−1\Sigma^{-1} divides the iith component by σi2\sigma_i^2. The Lyapunov equation, in turn, gives (R⊤ℒΣ(dΣ)R)ij=(R⊤dΣR)ijσi2+σj2.\bigl(R^{\top}\mathcal L_\Sigma(d\Sigma)R\bigr)_{ij} =\frac{(R^{\top}d\Sigma R)_{ij}}{\sigma_i^2+\sigma_j^2}. Thus the two covariance norms can be evaluated entry by entry: 12tr⁡(Σ−1dΣΣ−1dΣ)=12∑i,j=1n(R⊤dΣR)ij2σi2σj2,12tr⁡(ℒΣ(dΣ)dΣ)=12∑i,j=1n(R⊤dΣR)ij2σi2+σj2.\begin{aligned} \frac12\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\Sigma^{-1}d\Sigma\,\Sigma^{-1}d\Sigma) &=\frac12\sum_{i,j=1}^n \frac{(R^{\top}d\Sigma R)_{ij}^2}{\sigma_i^2\sigma_j^2},\\ \frac12\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\bigl(\mathcal L_\Sigma(d\Sigma)\,d\Sigma\bigr) &=\frac12\sum_{i,j=1}^n \frac{(R^{\top}d\Sigma R)_{ij}^2}{\sigma_i^2+\sigma_j^2}. \end{aligned} Substitution of the diagonal and off-diagonal entries yields the full line elements in rotation–scale coordinates: dsFR2=∑i=1n(R⊤dμ)i2σi2+2∑i=1ndσi2σi2+∑i<j(σi2−σj2)2σi2σj2Ωij2,\boxed{ \begin{aligned} ds_{\mathrm{FR}}^2 &=\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{(R^{\top}d\mu)_i^2}{\sigma_i^2} +2\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{d\sigma_i^2}{\sigma_i^2}\\ &\quad+\sum_{i<j} \frac{(\sigma_i^2-\sigma_j^2)^2}{\sigma_i^2\sigma_j^2} \Omega_{ij}^2, \end{aligned} } dsWO2=∥dμ∥2+∑i=1ndσi2+∑i<j(σi2−σj2)2σi2+σj2Ωij2.\boxed{ \begin{aligned} ds_{\mathrm{WO}}^2 &=\|d\mu\|^2+\sum_{i=1}^n d\sigma_i^2\\ &\quad+\sum_{i<j} \frac{(\sigma_i^2-\sigma_j^2)^2}{\sigma_i^2+\sigma_j^2} \Omega_{ij}^2. \end{aligned} } Here dσi2d\sigma_i^2 means (dσi)2(d\sigma_i)^2, the squared scale direction. Also, (R⊤dμ)i(R^{\top}d\mu)_i is the component of the mean direction in the current principal-axis basis. We have reparameterized only Σ\Sigma: R⊤dμR^{\top}d\mu is not being identified with the differential of R⊤μR^{\top}\mu, which would also contain a term involving dRdR.

6.4.2.1 Changes of principal scale.

Fix the orientation RR and the mean μ\mu. The two squared norms reduce to 2∑i(dσiσi)2for Fisher–Rao,∑i(dσi)2for Wasserstein–Otto.2\sum_i\left(\frac{d\sigma_i}{\sigma_i}\right)^2 \quad\text{for Fisher--Rao},\qquad \sum_i(d\sigma_i)^2 \quad\text{for Wasserstein--Otto}. Equal relative scale changes have equal Fisher cost, while equal absolute scale changes have equal transport cost. The scale part of Fisher–Rao is Euclidean in log⁡σi\log\sigma_i, up to the factor two; the transport scale part is Euclidean in σi\sigma_i. Figure 20 shows the corresponding distance maps in the ordered half-plane σ1>σ2>0\sigma_1>\sigma_2>0: Fisher–Rao contours become circles in logarithmic scale coordinates, while Wasserstein–Otto contours are ordinary circles.

6.4.2.2 Changes of orientation.

For a rotation mixing principal axes ii and jj, the two coefficients are (σi2−σj2)2σi2σj2and(σi2−σj2)2σi2+σj2.\frac{(\sigma_i^2-\sigma_j^2)^2}{\sigma_i^2\sigma_j^2} \quad\text{and}\quad \frac{(\sigma_i^2-\sigma_j^2)^2}{\sigma_i^2+\sigma_j^2}. The Fisher coefficient depends only on the ratio of the two scales. Multiplying all scales by the same positive factor leaves it unchanged. The transport coefficient is multiplied by the square of that factor: rotating a larger distribution moves its mass farther. In two dimensions, if RR is parameterized by an angle α\alpha, then Ω122=(dα)2\Omega_{12}^2=(d\alpha)^2, so these are directly the coefficients of the angular squared displacement. Figure 21 plots the resulting Fisher–Rao and Wasserstein–Otto distances as the orientation varies at fixed scales: the Fisher distance depends only on the scale ratio, while the transport distance grows with the absolute scales.

Both coefficients vanish when σi=σj\sigma_i=\sigma_j. A rotation within the corresponding eigenspace leaves Σ\Sigma unchanged, so it induces no density variation. This is a redundancy of the rotation–scale representation, not a degeneracy of either metric on Sn++(ℝ)S_n^{++}(\mathbb{R}). At repeated eigenvalues, these variables are not a regular coordinate chart; the original matrix formulas remain valid.

Scale and rotation directions are orthogonal in the displayed formulas. They are not independent geometries: the rotation coefficients depend on the principal scales. Consequently, linear interpolation of scales and rotation angles does not in general produce a geodesic of either full metric.

6.4.3 Geodesics in the Gaussian parameter space

All parameter paths below describe Gaussian densities pμ(t),Σ(t)p_{\mu(t),\Sigma(t)}. Their length is computed from the corresponding pullback metric on Θ\Theta. A Fisher–Rao geodesic here is intrinsic to the Gaussian family; it is not asserted to be a geodesic in the full space of densities equipped with the ambient Fisher–Rao metric.

6.4.3.1 Wasserstein–Otto: independent mean and covariance paths.

The mean block is Euclidean, and the covariance block depends only on Σ\Sigma, so this metric is a Riemannian product. For endpoints (μ0,Σ0)(\mu_0,\Sigma_0) and (μ1,Σ1)(\mu_1,\Sigma_1), define the symmetric positive-definite transport matrix T=Σ0−1/2(Σ01/2Σ1Σ01/2)1/2Σ0−1/2,TΣ0T=Σ1.T=\Sigma_0^{-1/2} (\Sigma_0^{1/2}\Sigma_1\Sigma_0^{1/2})^{1/2} \Sigma_0^{-1/2}, \qquad T\Sigma_0T=\Sigma_1. Matrix square roots here are the positive-definite square roots. The optimal affine transport sends xx to μ1+T(x−μ0)\mu_1+T(x-\mu_0). Its displacement interpolation remains Gaussian and gives the constant-speed geodesic [10] (Sections 3 and 4): μ(t)=(1−t)μ0+tμ1,Σ(t)=((1−t)I+tT)Σ0((1−t)I+tT),0≤t≤1.\boxed{ \begin{aligned} \mu(t)&=(1-t)\mu_0+t\mu_1,\\ \Sigma(t)&=((1-t)I+tT)\Sigma_0((1-t)I+tT), \qquad 0\leq t\leq1. \end{aligned} } Since (1−t)I+tT(1-t)I+tT is positive definite, the covariance remains positive definite. This path realizes both the intrinsic Gaussian distance and the ambient Wasserstein distance, with W22(pμ0,Σ0,pμ1,Σ1)=∥μ1−μ0∥2+tr⁡(Σ0+Σ1−2(Σ01/2Σ1Σ01/2)1/2).\begin{aligned} &W_2^2(p_{\mu_0,\Sigma_0},p_{\mu_1,\Sigma_1})\\ &\quad=\|\mu_1-\mu_0\|^2 +\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\!\left(\Sigma_0+\Sigma_1 -2(\Sigma_0^{1/2}\Sigma_1\Sigma_0^{1/2})^{1/2}\right). \end{aligned} The covariance path is generally not linear in the entries of Σ\Sigma. For the Gaussian distance formula above, see Dowson and Landau [18]. If the endpoint covariances commute, they admit a common principal-axis basis. In that basis the scales interpolate linearly: σi(t)=(1−t)σi,0+tσi,1.\sigma_i(t)=(1-t)\sigma_{i,0}+t\sigma_{i,1}. When the endpoint covariances do not commute, the matrix formula determines both the changing scales and orientation; neither is generally an affine function of time.

6.4.3.2 Fisher–Rao: coupling between mean and covariance.

For the full Gaussian family, the geodesic equations are [11] (Section 2.2) μ̈(t)=Σ̇(t)Σ(t)−1μ̇(t),Σ̈(t)=Σ̇(t)Σ(t)−1Σ̇(t)−μ̇(t)μ̇(t)⊤.\boxed{ \begin{aligned} \ddot\mu(t)&=\dot\Sigma(t)\Sigma(t)^{-1}\dot\mu(t),\\ \ddot\Sigma(t)&=\dot\Sigma(t)\Sigma(t)^{-1}\dot\Sigma(t) -\dot\mu(t)\dot\mu(t)^{\top}. \end{aligned} } Dots denote differentiation with respect to the geodesic parameter tt. These equations follow from the energy associated with the Fisher–Rao line element and display the coupling absent from the transport metric. A changing covariance affects the mean velocity, and a moving mean affects the covariance evolution. Even with identical endpoint covariances, a nonconstant mean path with covariance fixed throughout is not a geodesic: the second equation would require μ̇(t)μ̇(t)⊤=0\dot\mu(t)\dot\mu(t)^{\top}=0.

A minimizing path can instead broaden the distribution in directions of travel, reducing the mean cost before restoring the endpoint covariance. This already occurs in the one-dimensional Gaussian example. In higher dimensions, the mean path can also curve in response to anisotropy. One must solve the coupled equations with the endpoint conditions, rather than independently interpolate the mean, scales, and angles.

6.4.3.3 Fixed mean: an explicit covariance comparison.

When μ0=μ1\mu_0=\mu_1, a constant mean minimizes the mean contribution for any covariance path. The Fisher–Rao minimizing covariance path is [11] (Section 2.3) μ(t)=μ0,Σ(t)=Σ01/2(Σ0−1/2Σ1Σ0−1/2)tΣ01/2.\boxed{ \mu(t)=\mu_0,\qquad \Sigma(t)=\Sigma_0^{1/2} (\Sigma_0^{-1/2}\Sigma_1\Sigma_0^{-1/2})^t \Sigma_0^{1/2}. } Matrix powers are defined by applying the scalar power to the positive eigenvalues. Its length is dgFR((μ0,Σ0),(μ0,Σ1))=[12tr([log(Σ0−1/2Σ1Σ0−1/2)]2)]1/2.d_{g^{\mathrm{FR}}}\bigl((\mu_0,\Sigma_0),(\mu_0,\Sigma_1)\bigr) =\left[\frac12\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\!\left( \bigl[\log(\Sigma_0^{-1/2}\Sigma_1\Sigma_0^{-1/2})\bigr]^2 \right)\right]^{1/2}. The Fisher formula here requires a common mean. It must not be combined with an arbitrary moving mean to claim a geodesic of the full Gaussian family. Likewise, a path obtained by varying only the orientation while holding all scales fixed is a constrained path, not generally the minimizing path between its endpoints for either metric. 7 Gibbs families

A Gibbs family is a family of densities of the form pθ(x)=1Zθe−Uθ(x),Zθ=∫Ee−Uθ(x)dx,p_\theta(x)=\frac{1}{Z_\theta}e^{-U_\theta(x)}, \qquad Z_\theta=\int_E e^{-U_\theta(x)}\,dx, where UθU_\theta is the potential, or energy, and ZθZ_\theta is the normalization constant. We take E=ℝnE=\mathbb{R}^n and let Θ\Theta be an open subset of ℝk\mathbb{R}^k on which 0<Zθ<∞0<Z_\theta<\infty. We assume that the densities have finite second moments and that the potentials have the spatial and parameter regularity needed below. In particular, differentiation under the integral sign is justified, the parameter derivatives of UθU_\theta are square-integrable under pθp_\theta, and integration by parts has no boundary contribution at infinity. We write 𝔼θ[f]=∫Ef(x)pθ(x)dx\mathbb E_\theta[f]=\int_E f(x)p_\theta(x)\,dx.

The family is called linear when the potential is linear in the parameters: Uθ(x)=ϕ(x)Tθ,ϕ:E→ℝk.U_\theta(x)=\phi(x)^T\theta, \qquad \phi\colon E\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^k. The components of ϕ\phi may be nonlinear functions of xx: linearity here concerns θ\theta. We first allow a general nonlinear dependence θ↦Uθ\theta\mapsto U_\theta; the linear case will then follow by replacing ∇θUθ\nabla_\theta U_\theta with ϕ\phi. Figure 23 shows four two-parameter examples, two linear and two nonlinear, in one and two spatial dimensions.

7.1 Differentiation with respect to the parameters

Since Θ⊂ℝk\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^k is open, its tangent space is TθΘ≃ℝk.T_\theta\Theta\simeq\mathbb{R}^k. We use the parameter gradient ∇θ=(∂θ1,…,∂θk)T\nabla_\theta=(\partial_{\theta_1},\ldots,\partial_{\theta_k})^T; the spatial variable xx is held fixed. Applying the product rule directly to pθ(x)=Zθ−1e−Uθ(x)p_\theta(x)=Z_\theta^{-1}e^{-U_\theta(x)} gives ∇θpθ(x)=−∇θZθZθ2e−Uθ(x)−1Zθe−Uθ(x)∇θUθ(x)=pθ(x)(−∇θZθZθ−∇θUθ(x)).\begin{aligned} \nabla_\theta p_\theta(x) &=-\frac{\nabla_\theta Z_\theta}{Z_\theta^2} e^{-U_\theta(x)} -\frac{1}{Z_\theta}e^{-U_\theta(x)}\nabla_\theta U_\theta(x)\\ &=p_\theta(x)\left( -\frac{\nabla_\theta Z_\theta}{Z_\theta} -\nabla_\theta U_\theta(x)\right). \end{aligned} Differentiating the normalization under the integral sign, ∇θZθ=−∫E∇θUθ(x)e−Uθ(x)dx=−Zθ𝔼θ[∇θUθ].\begin{aligned} \nabla_\theta Z_\theta &=-\int_E\nabla_\theta U_\theta(x)e^{-U_\theta(x)}\,dx\\ &=-Z_\theta\,\mathbb E_\theta[\nabla_\theta U_\theta]. \end{aligned} Substitution therefore yields ∇θpθ(x)=−pθ(x)(∇θUθ(x)−𝔼θ[∇θUθ]).\boxed{ \nabla_\theta p_\theta(x) =-p_\theta(x)\bigl(\nabla_\theta U_\theta(x) -\mathbb E_\theta[\nabla_\theta U_\theta]\bigr). } For a tangent direction dθ∈TθΘd\theta\in T_\theta\Theta, the differential is obtained by pairing this gradient with dθd\theta: dθpθ(dθ)=(∇θpθ)Tdθ=−pθ(∇θUθ−𝔼θ[∇θUθ])Tdθ,dθUθ(dθ)=(∇θUθ)Tdθ.\begin{aligned} d_\theta p_\theta(d\theta) &=(\nabla_\theta p_\theta)^T d\theta\\ &=-p_\theta\bigl(\nabla_\theta U_\theta -\mathbb E_\theta[\nabla_\theta U_\theta]\bigr)^T d\theta, \qquad d_\theta U_\theta(d\theta)=(\nabla_\theta U_\theta)^T d\theta. \end{aligned} Its integral vanishes, as required for a tangent density variation. Normalization therefore removes the mean energy variation: only its fluctuations change the density. In particular, adding a parameter-dependent constant to Uθ(x)U_\theta(x) changes ZθZ_\theta but leaves pθp_\theta unchanged.

It is generally variable, even though the potential is linear in those parameters.

7.3 Wasserstein–Otto metric

7.3.1 From the density variation to the transport potential

Fix θ\theta and a parameter direction dθd\theta. The corresponding density variation is u=dθpθ(dθ)u=d_\theta p_\theta(d\theta). We seek its minimal-energy velocity as a spatial gradient, as in [12] (Section 3.1), v(x)=∇xψθ,dθ(x),−div⁡x(pθ∇xψθ,dθ)=u.v(x)=\nabla_x\psi_{\theta,d\theta}(x), \qquad -\operatorname{div}_x(p_\theta\nabla_x\psi_{\theta,d\theta})=u. The potential ψθ,dθ\psi_{\theta,d\theta} depends linearly on the tangent direction once its additive constant is fixed. We choose 𝔼θ[ψθ,dθ]=0\mathbb E_\theta[\psi_{\theta,d\theta}]=0. To ensure a well-defined finite-energy solution for every parameter direction, we assume a weighted Poincaré inequality at each parameter: 𝔼θ[(f−𝔼θf)2]≤1λθ𝔼θ[∥∇xf∥2],λθ>0.\mathbb E_\theta[(f-\mathbb E_\theta f)^2] \leq\frac{1}{\lambda_\theta} \mathbb E_\theta[\|\nabla_x f\|^2], \qquad \lambda_\theta>0. Together with the square-integrability assumptions, this gives existence and uniqueness of the centered weak solution in the weighted Sobolev space. This is an additional assumption on the Gibbs family, not a consequence of normalization alone.

Substitution of the density differential gives −div⁡x(pθ∇xψθ,dθ)=−pθ(dθUθ(dθ)−𝔼θ[dθUθ(dθ)]).-\operatorname{div}_x(p_\theta\nabla_x\psi_{\theta,d\theta}) =-p_\theta\bigl(d_\theta U_\theta(d\theta) -\mathbb E_\theta[d_\theta U_\theta(d\theta)]\bigr). The normalization ZθZ_\theta is constant with respect to xx, hence ∇xpθ=−pθ∇xUθ.\nabla_x p_\theta=-p_\theta\nabla_x U_\theta. Expanding the divergence and dividing by pθ>0p_\theta>0 therefore yields Δxψθ,dθ−∇xUθ⋅∇xψθ,dθ=dθUθ(dθ)−𝔼θ[dθUθ(dθ)].\boxed{ \Delta_x\psi_{\theta,d\theta} -\nabla_x U_\theta\cdot\nabla_x\psi_{\theta,d\theta} =d_\theta U_\theta(d\theta) -\mathbb E_\theta[d_\theta U_\theta(d\theta)]. } On the left, all derivatives act on the spatial variable xx. On the right, dθUθ(dθ)d_\theta U_\theta(d\theta) differentiates the potential with respect to its parameters. The two operations have different roles: the parameter variation prescribes a density change, and the spatial Poisson equation reconstructs the velocity that produces it.

It is useful to write the positive weighted operator as Lθ(f):=−1pθdiv⁡x(pθ∇xf)=−Δxf+∇xUθ⋅∇xf.L_\theta(f) :=-\frac{1}{p_\theta}\operatorname{div}_x(p_\theta\nabla_x f) =-\Delta_x f+\nabla_x U_\theta\cdot\nabla_x f. Integration by parts gives 𝔼θ[fLθ(h)]=𝔼θ[∇xf⋅∇xh].\mathbb E_\theta[f\,L_\theta(h)] =\mathbb E_\theta[\nabla_x f\cdot\nabla_x h]. Thus LθL_\theta is symmetric and nonnegative in L2(pθdx)L^2(p_\theta dx), with constants in its kernel. Its inverse below is always taken on the centered subspace, where the Poincaré inequality makes it well defined. With this sign convention, ψθ,dθ=−Lθ−1(dθUθ(dθ)−𝔼θ[dθUθ(dθ)]).\psi_{\theta,d\theta} =-L_\theta^{-1}\!\left( d_\theta U_\theta(d\theta) -\mathbb E_\theta[d_\theta U_\theta(d\theta)]\right).

7.3.2 The pullback metric and the linear case

The Wasserstein–Otto metric is the energy of these velocities: gθWO(dθ1,dθ2)=𝔼θ[∇xψθ,dθ1⋅∇xψθ,dθ2].\boxed{ g_\theta^{\mathrm{WO}}(d\theta_1,d\theta_2) =\mathbb E_\theta\!\left[ \nabla_x\psi_{\theta,d\theta_1} \cdot\nabla_x\psi_{\theta,d\theta_2}\right]. } To compute its matrix, let ψθ,i\psi_{\theta,i} be the centered solution for variation in the iith parameter: Lθ(ψθ,i)=−(∂θiUθ−𝔼θ[∂θiUθ]).L_\theta(\psi_{\theta,i}) =-\bigl(\partial_{\theta_i}U_\theta -\mathbb E_\theta[\partial_{\theta_i}U_\theta]\bigr). Then ψθ,dθ=∑idθiψθ,i\psi_{\theta,d\theta}=\sum_i d\theta_i\psi_{\theta,i} and GijWO(θ)=𝔼θ[∇xψθ,i⋅∇xψθ,j]=𝔼θ[ψθ,iLθ(ψθ,j)]=−𝔼θ[ψθ,i(∂θjUθ−𝔼θ[∂θjUθ])].\begin{aligned} G^{\mathrm{WO}}_{ij}(\theta) &=\mathbb E_\theta[ \nabla_x\psi_{\theta,i}\cdot\nabla_x\psi_{\theta,j}]\\ &=\mathbb E_\theta[\psi_{\theta,i}L_\theta(\psi_{\theta,j})]\\ &=-\mathbb E_\theta\!\left[ \psi_{\theta,i} \bigl(\partial_{\theta_j}U_\theta -\mathbb E_\theta[\partial_{\theta_j}U_\theta]\bigr)\right]. \end{aligned} Equivalently, moving the inverse operator onto the centered energy variation gives GijWO(θ)=𝔼θ[(∂θiUθ−𝔼θ[∂θiUθ])Lθ−1(∂θjUθ−𝔼θ[∂θjUθ])].\boxed{ \begin{aligned} G^{\mathrm{WO}}_{ij}(\theta) =\mathbb E_\theta\!\Big[& \bigl(\partial_{\theta_i}U_\theta -\mathbb E_\theta[\partial_{\theta_i}U_\theta]\bigr)\\[-2pt] &L_\theta^{-1} \bigl(\partial_{\theta_j}U_\theta -\mathbb E_\theta[\partial_{\theta_j}U_\theta]\bigr) \Big]. \end{aligned} } The two minus signs from the density variations have cancelled. The operator acts on functions of xx at the current parameter θ\theta; it is not an inverse of the Fisher matrix on Θ\Theta.

For Uθ=ϕTθU_\theta=\phi^T\theta, this becomes GijWO(θ)=𝔼θ[(ϕi−𝔼θϕi)Lθ−1(ϕj−𝔼θϕj)].\boxed{ G^{\mathrm{WO}}_{ij}(\theta) =\mathbb E_\theta\!\left[ (\phi_i-\mathbb E_\theta\phi_i) L_\theta^{-1}(\phi_j-\mathbb E_\theta\phi_j) \right]. } The source functions are now the centered sufficient statistics, but LθL_\theta still depends on the parameter through Uθ=∑iθiϕiU_\theta=\sum_i\theta_i\phi_i. In particular, linearity of the energy does not remove the Poisson solve. Numerically, one solves kk centered Poisson problems at each parameter and takes their gradient inner products to obtain the metric matrix.

7.4 Comparison and geodesics

7.4.1 Fluctuation cost and transport cost

Both pullbacks act on the same vector dθ∈TθΘd\theta\in T_\theta\Theta and on the same density differential. To display their difference, denote this single centered energy variation by hθ(dθ):=dθUθ(dθ)−𝔼θ[dθUθ(dθ)].h_\theta(d\theta) :=d_\theta U_\theta(d\theta) -\mathbb E_\theta[d_\theta U_\theta(d\theta)]. The two squared norms are gθFR(dθ,dθ)=𝔼θ[hθ(dθ)2],gθWO(dθ,dθ)=𝔼θ[hθ(dθ)Lθ−1(hθ(dθ))].\boxed{ \begin{aligned} g_\theta^{\mathrm{FR}}(d\theta,d\theta) &=\mathbb E_\theta[h_\theta(d\theta)^2],\\ g_\theta^{\mathrm{WO}}(d\theta,d\theta) &=\mathbb E_\theta\!\left[ h_\theta(d\theta)\, L_\theta^{-1}\bigl(h_\theta(d\theta)\bigr)\right]. \end{aligned} } Fisher–Rao measures the size of the relative density change: dθpθ(dθ)pθ=−hθ(dθ).\frac{d_\theta p_\theta(d\theta)}{p_\theta}=-h_\theta(d\theta). Wasserstein–Otto also accounts for the spatial arrangement of this change: it measures the least kinetic energy needed to redistribute mass in EE. Two energy variations with equal variance can therefore have different transport costs.

For example, if a centered spatial function hh satisfies Lθ(h)=λhL_\theta(h)=\lambda h with λ>0\lambda>0, then a direction with hθ(dθ)=hh_\theta(d\theta)=h has gθWO(dθ,dθ)=1λgθFR(dθ,dθ).g_\theta^{\mathrm{WO}}(d\theta,d\theta) =\frac{1}{\lambda}\, g_\theta^{\mathrm{FR}}(d\theta,d\theta). More generally, whenever the centered operator admits a complete orthonormal eigenbasis, the same expansion gives a sum of squared mode coefficients for Fisher–Rao, and the same sum weighted by inverse eigenvalues for Wasserstein–Otto. Small eigenvalues correspond to spatial modes that are expensive to realize by transport. This explains why the two matrices on Θ\Theta need not have the same principal directions or relative eigenvalues.

The Poincaré inequality provides the local comparison gθWO(dθ,dθ)≤λθ−1gθFR(dθ,dθ).g_\theta^{\mathrm{WO}}(d\theta,d\theta) \leq\lambda_\theta^{-1} g_\theta^{\mathrm{FR}}(d\theta,d\theta). Indeed, writing f=Lθ−1(hθ(dθ))f=L_\theta^{-1}\bigl(h_\theta(d\theta)\bigr), integration by parts, Cauchy–Schwarz and Poincaré give 𝔼θ[∥∇xf∥2]=𝔼θ[hθ(dθ)f]≤(𝔼θ[hθ(dθ)2])1/2(λθ−1𝔼θ[∥∇xf∥2])1/2.\begin{aligned} \mathbb E_\theta[\|\nabla_x f\|^2] &=\mathbb E_\theta[h_\theta(d\theta)f]\\ &\leq\bigl(\mathbb E_\theta[h_\theta(d\theta)^2]\bigr)^{1/2} \bigl(\lambda_\theta^{-1} \mathbb E_\theta[\|\nabla_x f\|^2]\bigr)^{1/2}. \end{aligned} The comparison factor depends on the current law and spatial scale; there is no universal ordering of the two squared norms with factor one.

Their null directions nevertheless coincide. Either norm vanishes exactly when dθUθ(dθ)d_\theta U_\theta(d\theta) is constant pθp_\theta-almost everywhere, equivalently when dθpθ(dθ)=0d_\theta p_\theta(d\theta)=0. Under the stated existence assumptions, both pullbacks are therefore positive definite precisely where θ↦pθ\theta\mapsto p_\theta is an immersion. In the linear case, this means that no nonzero linear combination of the ϕi\phi_i is constant almost everywhere. When the matrices are positive definite, their unit ellipsoids dθTG(θ)dθ=1d\theta^TG(\theta)d\theta=1 describe which parameter variations are locally costly for each metric.

7.4.2 Intrinsic geodesics of the Gibbs family

A curve is now a path t↦θ(t)t\mapsto\theta(t) in Θ\Theta, representing the densities pθ(t)p_{\theta(t)}. Its length and energy for either metric are Lg[θ]=∫01θ̇(t)TG(θ(t))θ̇(t)dt,ℰg[θ]=12∫01θ̇(t)TG(θ(t))θ̇(t)dt.\begin{aligned} L_g[\theta] &=\int_0^1\sqrt{\dot\theta(t)^TG(\theta(t))\dot\theta(t)}\,dt,\\ \mathcal E_g[\theta] &=\frac12\int_0^1\dot\theta(t)^TG(\theta(t))\dot\theta(t)\,dt. \end{aligned} On a regular parameter region where the metric is sufficiently smooth, constant-speed geodesics are stationary paths of this energy. The coordinate equations are ddt(∑j=1kGij(θ(t))θ̇j(t))=12∑j,ℓ=1k∂θiGjℓ(θ(t))θ̇j(t)θ̇ℓ(t),i=1,…,k.\frac{d}{dt}\!\left( \sum_{j=1}^kG_{ij}(\theta(t))\dot\theta_j(t)\right) =\frac12\sum_{j,\ell=1}^k \partial_{\theta_i}G_{j\ell}(\theta(t)) \dot\theta_j(t)\dot\theta_\ell(t), \qquad i=1,\ldots,k. The intrinsic distance is the infimum of LgL_g over paths in Θ\Theta with the prescribed endpoints. A minimizing constant-speed path, when it exists, satisfies these equations; a stationary solution alone need not be globally minimizing.

For Fisher–Rao, the energy integrates the variance of the instantaneous energy variation dθUθ(t)(θ̇(t))d_\theta U_{\theta(t)}(\dot\theta(t)). For Wasserstein–Otto, the same variation prescribes a Poisson problem at each time, and the energy integrates the resulting squared velocity. Thus the path selection differs even when both metrics have the same null directions. Neither metric generally makes straight parameter segments geodesic.

In particular, the linear Gibbs case gives GFR=Hess⁡log⁡ZG^{\mathrm{FR}}=\operatorname{Hess}\log Z, but a Hessian metric need not be constant or Riemannian-flat. Straight lines in natural parameters are the exponential interpolation paths of the family; they are not, in general, its Levi–Civita geodesics [14] (Sections 3.8–3.9). The Hessian structure also does not transfer automatically to GWOG^{\mathrm{WO}}, which includes the parameter-dependent inverse spatial operator.

Finally, these are intrinsic geodesics of the chosen Gibbs family. The ambient Fisher–Rao geodesic obtained in square-root density coordinates, or the ambient Wasserstein displacement interpolation, need not remain in that family. Their distances are lower bounds for the corresponding intrinsic distances, with equality when an ambient minimizing path is realized by a path in the family. In the general nonlinear case, neither normalization nor the Gibbs form alone provides a closed formula for these parameter-space geodesics or their curvature.

8 Push-forward families

8.1 Transporting a reference density

Let qq be a fixed positive probability density on E=ℝnE=\mathbb{R}^n, and let Θ⊂ℝk\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^k be open. A push-forward family is obtained by transporting this reference law through parameter-dependent maps ϕθ:E→E,pθ=(ϕθ)♯q.\phi_\theta\colon E\longrightarrow E, \qquad p_\theta=(\phi_\theta)_\sharp q. Thus, if XX has density qq, then ϕθ(X)\phi_\theta(X) has density pθp_\theta. We assume that each ϕθ\phi_\theta is a diffeomorphism, with sufficient regularity in both its spatial variable and its parameters. The change-of-variables formula reads pθ(y)=q(ϕθ−1(y))|det⁡Dxϕθ(ϕθ−1(y))|.\boxed{ p_\theta(y) =\frac{q(\phi_\theta^{-1}(y))} {|\det D_x\phi_\theta(\phi_\theta^{-1}(y))|}. } Here DxϕθD_x\phi_\theta denotes the spatial Jacobian matrix. Equivalently, pθ(ϕθ(x))|det⁡Dxϕθ(x)|=q(x).p_\theta(\phi_\theta(x))\,|\det D_x\phi_\theta(x)|=q(x). The variable xx labels a reference point carrying the density qq; y=ϕθ(x)y=\phi_\theta(x) is its current position, where the transported density is evaluated. These two variables must be distinguished when we differentiate with respect to θ\theta.

We assume that ∫E∥ϕθ(x)∥2q(x)dx<∞\int_E\|\phi_\theta(x)\|^2q(x)\,dx<\infty, so pθ∈𝒫2,+(E)p_\theta\in\mathcal P_{2,+}(E), and that the derivatives and weighted integrals below exist. Integration by parts is understood with decay conditions eliminating boundary terms. The finite-energy Poisson solutions used for Wasserstein–Otto are also assumed to exist; as in Section 7.3, a weighted Poincaré inequality is a sufficient condition when the sources divided by pθp_\theta are square-integrable.

Affine maps already recover several familiar examples. A standard Gaussian reference and ϕμ,B(x)=μ+Bx\phi_{\mu,B}(x)=\mu+Bx, with BB invertible, give a Gaussian with mean μ\mu and covariance BBTBB^T. Nonlinear maps can bend the reference density: in dimension two, ϕθ(x1,x2)=(x1,θ1x12+θ2x2),θ1∈ℝ,θ2>0,\phi_\theta(x_1,x_2) =(x_1,\,\theta_1x_1^2+\theta_2x_2), \qquad \theta_1\in\mathbb{R},\quad\theta_2>0, is a diffeomorphism with determinant θ2\theta_2. Applied to a standard Gaussian reference, it gives the curved Gibbs density illustrated in Figure 23. A single density family can therefore admit both an energy description and a transport description.

8.2 Differentiation with respect to the parameters

We distinguish two differentiation operations. The notation DxD_x denotes the differential with respect to the spatial variable xx, with the parameter fixed; dθd_\theta denotes the differential with respect to the parameter, with the spatial point fixed. In particular, we set Jθ(x):=Dxϕθ(x),(Jθ(x))ab=∂xb(ϕθ)a(x).J_\theta(x):=D_x\phi_\theta(x), \qquad (J_\theta(x))_{ab}=\partial_{x_b}(\phi_\theta)_a(x). The matrix Jθ(x)J_\theta(x) is the spatial Jacobian, but it still depends on θ\theta. Since TθΘ≃ℝkT_\theta\Theta\simeq\mathbb{R}^k, a parameter direction dθd\theta gives the vector dθϕθ(dθ)(x)=∑i=1k∂θiϕθ(x)dθi.d_\theta\phi_\theta(d\theta)(x) =\sum_{i=1}^k\partial_{\theta_i}\phi_\theta(x)\,d\theta_i. For a scalar function of the current position yy, its spatial differential is represented by the gradient: Dypθ(y)(a)=∇ypθ(y)⋅aD_y p_\theta(y)(a)=\nabla_y p_\theta(y)\cdot a for a∈ℝna\in\mathbb{R}^n. The density variation we seek is dθpθ(dθ)(y)d_\theta p_\theta(d\theta)(y), where yy is held fixed.

8.2.0.1 Start from the density formula.

At a fixed reference point xx, the change-of-variables identity is pθ(ϕθ(x))=q(x)|det⁡Jθ(x)|.p_\theta(\phi_\theta(x)) =\frac{q(x)}{|\det J_\theta(x)|}. We differentiate each side along θ(s)=θ+sdθ\theta(s)=\theta+s\,d\theta at s=0s=0, holding xx and hence q(x)q(x) fixed.

8.2.0.2 Differentiate the right-hand side.

Only the denominator depends on θ\theta. Substitution gives dds|s=0q(x)|det⁡Jθ(s)(x)|=−q(x)|det⁡Jθ(x)|tr⁡(Jθ(x)−1dθJθ(dθ)(x))=−pθ(ϕθ(x))tr⁡(Jθ(x)−1dθJθ(dθ)(x)).\begin{aligned} \left.\frac{d}{ds}\right|_{s=0} \frac{q(x)}{|\det J_{\theta(s)}(x)|} &=-\frac{q(x)}{|\det J_\theta(x)|}\, \operatorname{tr}\!\left( J_\theta(x)^{-1}d_\theta J_\theta(d\theta)(x)\right)\\ &=-p_\theta(\phi_\theta(x))\, \operatorname{tr}\!\left( J_\theta(x)^{-1}d_\theta J_\theta(d\theta)(x)\right). \end{aligned} The last line substitutes pθ(ϕθ(x))=q(x)/|det⁡Jθ(x)|p_\theta(\phi_\theta(x))=q(x)/|\det J_\theta(x)|.

8.2.0.3 Differentiate the left-hand side.

Here there are two dependencies on θ\theta: the density pθp_\theta changes, and its evaluation point ϕθ(x)\phi_\theta(x) changes. The second differentiates its evaluation point, with the density function held fixed. Equating the derivatives of the two sides and isolating the first term gives dθpθ(dθ)(ϕθ(x))=−∇ypθ(ϕθ(x))⋅dθϕθ(dθ)(x)−pθ(ϕθ(x))tr⁡(Jθ(x)−1dθJθ(dθ)(x)).\begin{aligned} d_\theta p_\theta(d\theta)(\phi_\theta(x)) ={}&-\nabla_y p_\theta(\phi_\theta(x)) \cdot d_\theta\phi_\theta(d\theta)(x)\\ &-p_\theta(\phi_\theta(x))\, \operatorname{tr}\!\left( J_\theta(x)^{-1}d_\theta J_\theta(d\theta)(x)\right). \end{aligned}

8.2.0.4 Express the parameter derivative of the Jacobian.

Since Jθ=DxϕθJ_\theta=D_x\phi_\theta, the assumed C2C^2 regularity allows us to exchange the spatial and parameter derivatives: dθ(Dxϕθ)=Dx(dθϕθ).d_\theta(D_x\phi_\theta)=D_x(d_\theta\phi_\theta). The density differential above contains both motion through the density profile and change of the volume element; the velocity formulation will combine them.

8.3 The induced spatial velocity

The trajectory of a reference point under the parameter path θ+sdθ\theta+s\,d\theta has velocity dθϕθ(dθ)(x)d_\theta \phi_\theta(d\theta)(x) at s=0s=0. The integral of uu vanishes, but the weighted divergence need not vanish pointwise. Along a parameter path θ(t)\theta(t), the same formula reads ∂tpθ(t)+div⁡y(pθ(t)vθ(t),θ̇(t))=0.\partial_t p_{\theta(t)} +\operatorname{div}_y\bigl( p_{\theta(t)}v_{\theta(t),\dot\theta(t)}\bigr)=0. 8.4 Wasserstein–Otto metric

The map-induced field realizes the correct density variation, but it need not be a gradient and need not minimize kinetic energy. Applying the weighted Helmholtz decomposition of Section 4.2.3, write vθ,dθ=∇yψθ,dθ+wθ,dθ,div⁡y(pθwθ,dθ)=0.v_{\theta,d\theta}=\nabla_y\psi_{\theta,d\theta} +w_{\theta,d\theta}, \qquad \operatorname{div}_y(p_\theta w_{\theta,d\theta})=0. The two terms are orthogonal in L2(pθdy;ℝn)L^2(p_\theta dy;\mathbb{R}^n). The centered potential, fixed by ∫Eψθ,dθpθdy=0\int_E\psi_{\theta,d\theta}p_\theta\,dy=0, solves −div⁡y(pθ∇yψθ,dθ)=−div⁡y(pθvθ,dθ)=dθpθ(dθ).\boxed{ -\operatorname{div}_y(p_\theta\nabla_y\psi_{\theta,d\theta}) =-\operatorname{div}_y(p_\theta v_{\theta,d\theta}) =d_\theta p_\theta(d\theta). } Its gradient is the minimum-energy velocity for this density variation. The pullback metric is therefore gθWO(dθ1,dθ2)=∫Epθ∇yψθ,dθ1⋅∇yψθ,dθ2dy.\boxed{ g_\theta^{\mathrm{WO}}(d\theta_1,d\theta_2) =\int_E p_\theta\, \nabla_y\psi_{\theta,d\theta_1} \cdot\nabla_y\psi_{\theta,d\theta_2}\,dy. } For coordinate computations, set vθ,i(y)=∂θiϕθ(ϕθ−1(y)),i=1,…,k,v_{\theta,i}(y) =\partial_{\theta_i}\phi_\theta(\phi_\theta^{-1}(y)), \qquad i=1,\ldots,k, and let ψθ,i\psi_{\theta,i} solve the same Poisson equation with vθ,iv_{\theta,i} on its right-hand side. Linearity in dθd\theta gives GijWO(θ)=∫Epθ∇yψθ,i⋅∇yψθ,jdy.G^{\mathrm{WO}}_{ij}(\theta) =\int_E p_\theta\, \nabla_y\psi_{\theta,i}\cdot\nabla_y\psi_{\theta,j}\,dy.

The distinction between the prescribed map and the density is visible in the energy identity ∫Eq(x)∥dθϕθ(dθ)(x)∥2dx=∫Epθ(y)∥vθ,dθ(y)∥2dy=gθWO(dθ,dθ)+∫Epθ(y)∥wθ,dθ(y)∥2dy.\begin{aligned} \int_Eq(x)\|d_\theta \phi_\theta(d\theta)(x)\|^2\,dx &=\int_Ep_\theta(y)\|v_{\theta,d\theta}(y)\|^2\,dy\\ &=g_\theta^{\mathrm{WO}}(d\theta,d\theta) +\int_Ep_\theta(y)\|w_{\theta,d\theta}(y)\|^2\,dy. \end{aligned} Thus the squared L2(q)L^2(q) norm of the map variation is an upper bound for the density’s Wasserstein–Otto squared norm. They coincide exactly when the map-induced velocity has no weighted divergence-free component. In particular, when every coordinate velocity is an admissible gradient, GijWO(θ)=∫Eq(x)∂θiϕθ(x)⋅∂θjϕθ(x)dx.G^{\mathrm{WO}}_{ij}(\theta) =\int_Eq(x)\, \partial_{\theta_i}\phi_\theta(x) \cdot\partial_{\theta_j}\phi_\theta(x)\,dx. This expression applies only when the coordinate velocities are admissible gradients.

8.5 Fisher–Rao metric

Here the density differential can be inserted directly. For the detailed calculation and interpretation, see P. Guilbert, Pullback Fisher–Rao Geometries of Parametric Probability Families: An Expository Atlas, Sections 8.1–8.3 [1]. The formula is gθFR(dθ1,dθ2)=∫Ediv⁡y(pθvθ,dθ1)div⁡y(pθvθ,dθ2)pθdy.\boxed{ g_\theta^{\mathrm{FR}}(d\theta_1,d\theta_2) =\int_E \frac{\operatorname{div}_y(p_\theta v_{\theta,d\theta_1}) \operatorname{div}_y(p_\theta v_{\theta,d\theta_2})} {p_\theta}\,dy. } In coordinates, this gives GijFR(θ)=∫Ediv⁡y(pθvθ,i)div⁡y(pθvθ,j)pθdy.G^{\mathrm{FR}}_{ij}(\theta) =\int_E\frac{ \operatorname{div}_y(p_\theta v_{\theta,i}) \operatorname{div}_y(p_\theta v_{\theta,j})}{p_\theta}\,dy. The corresponding squared norm can also be read as gθFR(dθ,dθ)=∫Epθ(div⁡yvθ,dθ+∇ypθpθ⋅vθ,dθ)2dy.g_\theta^{\mathrm{FR}}(d\theta,d\theta) =\int_Ep_\theta\left( \operatorname{div}_y v_{\theta,d\theta} +\frac{\nabla_y p_\theta}{p_\theta} \cdot v_{\theta,d\theta}\right)^2\,dy. The expression inside parentheses has zero mean under pθp_\theta, since its integral against pθp_\theta is the integral of a divergence. Fisher–Rao therefore measures the variance of this relative compression and advection rate. It does not measure div⁡v\operatorname{div}v alone: even a spatially constant translation field can change a nonuniform density. The relation between Fisher–Rao geometry and metrics on diffeomorphism groups is developed, for compact sample manifolds, in [13] (Section 3.3).

8.6 Comparison of the metrics

8.6.0.1 What is being compared?

Fix a parameter θ\theta and a direction dθd\theta. The map gives the velocity vθ,dθv_{\theta,d\theta}, and the continuity equation gives the density variation u=−div⁡y(pθvθ,dθ).u=-\operatorname{div}_y(p_\theta v_{\theta,d\theta}). The two squared costs answer different questions: gθFR(dθ,dθ)=∫Epθ(upθ)2dy,gθWO(dθ,dθ)=min−div⁡y(pθa)=u∫Epθ∥a∥2dy.\begin{aligned} g_\theta^{\mathrm{FR}}(d\theta,d\theta) &=\int_E p_\theta\left(\frac{u}{p_\theta}\right)^2\,dy,\\ g_\theta^{\mathrm{WO}}(d\theta,d\theta) &=\min_{-\operatorname{div}_y(p_\theta a)=u} \int_Ep_\theta\|a\|^2\,dy. \end{aligned} Fisher–Rao asks how large the relative density change u/pθu/p_\theta is at each spatial point. Wasserstein–Otto asks how much kinetic energy is needed to produce that change by moving mass. Its minimization is over spatial velocity fields aa, not over parameter directions. The following situations separate the effects of motion, compression, spatial scale, and the choice of a velocity realizing uu.

8.6.0.2 1. Motion that leaves the density unchanged.

Take the standard isotropic Gaussian on ℝ2\mathbb{R}^2 and let ϕα\phi_\alpha be a rotation through angle α\alpha. The density remains the same for every α\alpha. The induced field is nevertheless nonzero: vα,dα(y)=dα(−y2,y1).v_{\alpha,d\alpha}(y)=d\alpha\,(-y_2,y_1). Its ordinary divergence is zero, and it is tangent to the circular density contours. Therefore ∇yp⋅vα,dα=0\nabla_y p\cdot v_{\alpha,d\alpha}=0 as well, and u=0,gαFR(dα,dα)=0,gαWO(dα,dα)=0.u=0,\qquad g^{\mathrm{FR}}_\alpha(d\alpha,d\alpha)=0,\qquad g^{\mathrm{WO}}_\alpha(d\alpha,d\alpha)=0. Figure 8 shows this density-preserving motion. Both metric costs vanish: u=0u=0, and zero velocity realizes it. The prescribed rotation nevertheless has kinetic energy ∫p∥vα,dα∥2=2(dα)2\int p\|v_{\alpha,d\alpha}\|^2=2(d\alpha)^2.

This is the precise meaning of the invariance with respect to motions that preserve density: adding any ww with div⁡y(pθw)=0\operatorname{div}_y(p_\theta w)=0 to a velocity leaves uu, and hence both metric costs, unchanged. It can change the energy of that particular velocity. Both pullbacks consequently have the same kernel, {dθ:div⁡y(pθvθ,dθ)=0}.\left\{d\theta: \operatorname{div}_y(p_\theta v_{\theta,d\theta})=0\right\}. Under the stated regularity assumptions, they are positive definite exactly when the parameter-to-density map is an immersion. Distinct transport maps alone need not give distinct densities.

8.6.0.3 2. Translation: motion without volume compression.

For ϕμ(x)=x+μ\phi_\mu(x)=x+\mu, the density is pμ(y)=q(y−μ)p_\mu(y)=q(y-\mu) and the velocity is the constant field vμ,dμ=dμv_{\mu,d\mu}=d\mu. Thus div⁡yvμ,dμ=0,u(y)=−∇ypμ(y)⋅dμ.\operatorname{div}_y v_{\mu,d\mu}=0, \qquad u(y)=-\nabla_y p_\mu(y)\cdot d\mu. Unlike the rotation above, this motion crosses the density contours. The density at a fixed location changes even though small volumes are not compressed. The velocity is already a gradient, giving gμWO(dμ,dμ)=∥dμ∥2,gμFR(dμ,dμ)=∫E(∇xq(x)⋅dμ)2q(x)dx.\begin{aligned} g_\mu^{\mathrm{WO}}(d\mu,d\mu)&=\|d\mu\|^2,\\ g_\mu^{\mathrm{FR}}(d\mu,d\mu) &=\int_E\frac{(\nabla_xq(x)\cdot d\mu)^2}{q(x)}\,dx. \end{aligned} For a Gaussian reference with covariance Σ\Sigma, these become gμWO(dμ,dμ)=∥dμ∥2,gμFR(dμ,dμ)=dμTΣ−1dμ.g_\mu^{\mathrm{WO}}(d\mu,d\mu)=\|d\mu\|^2, \qquad g_\mu^{\mathrm{FR}}(d\mu,d\mu)=d\mu^T\Sigma^{-1}d\mu. Wasserstein–Otto charges for the displacement itself. Fisher–Rao charges more for the same displacement when the density is narrow in that direction: its profile changes more sharply at fixed spatial points. Figure 25 compares a narrow and a broad Gaussian under the same translational dμd\mu: the Wasserstein–Otto integrals coincide, while the Fisher–Rao cost is larger for the narrow density.

8.6.0.4 3. Dilation: absolute and relative scale changes.

Take q=𝒩(0,In)q=\mathcal N(0,I_n) and ϕσ(x)=σx\phi_\sigma(x)=\sigma x, with σ>0\sigma>0. Then pσ=𝒩(0,σ2In)p_\sigma=\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2I_n) and vσ,dσ(y)=dσσy,u(y)pσ(y)=dσσ(∥y∥2σ2−n).v_{\sigma,d\sigma}(y)=\frac{d\sigma}{\sigma}y, \qquad \frac{u(y)}{p_\sigma(y)} =\frac{d\sigma}{\sigma} \left(\frac{\|y\|^2}{\sigma^2}-n\right). The field is a gradient. Using 𝔼[∥Y∥2]=nσ2\mathbb E[\|Y\|^2]=n\sigma^2 and Var⁡(∥Y∥2/σ2)=2n\operatorname{Var}(\|Y\|^2/\sigma^2)=2n, we obtain gσFR(dσ,dσ)=2n(dσσ)2,gσWO(dσ,dσ)=n(dσ)2.\boxed{ g_\sigma^{\mathrm{FR}}(d\sigma,d\sigma) =2n\left(\frac{d\sigma}{\sigma}\right)^2, \qquad g_\sigma^{\mathrm{WO}}(d\sigma,d\sigma)=n(d\sigma)^2. } For the same absolute increment dσd\sigma, Fisher–Rao charges more at small σ\sigma, while the WO cost is independent of σ\sigma. For the same relative increment dσ/σd\sigma/\sigma, the Fisher cost is constant, while the WO cost increases with σ2\sigma^2: a proportional expansion of a larger distribution moves mass farther.

8.6.0.5 4. Shear: why the induced velocity must be projected.

In dimension two, let q=𝒩(0,I2)q=\mathcal N(0,I_2) and ϕθ(x1,x2)=(x1+θx2,x2)\phi_\theta(x_1,x_2)=(x_1+\theta x_2,x_2). At θ=0\theta=0, p0=qp_0=q and v0,dθ(y)=dθ(y2,0),u(y)=dθy1y2q(y).v_{0,d\theta}(y)=d\theta\,(y_2,0), \qquad u(y)=d\theta\,y_1y_2q(y). Again the ordinary divergence is zero, but the weighted divergence is not. The shear deforms the circular density contours. Its velocity splits explicitly as v0,dθ(y)=dθ2(y2,y1)+dθ2(y2,−y1).v_{0,d\theta}(y) =\frac{d\theta}{2}(y_2,y_1) +\frac{d\theta}{2}(y_2,-y_1). The first term is the gradient of dθy1y2/2d\theta\,y_1y_2/2. The second is a rotational field with zero qq-weighted divergence. Only the first is needed to produce uu. Since the standard Gaussian coordinates are independent with unit variance, g0FR(dθ,dθ)=(dθ)2𝔼q[Y12Y22]=(dθ)2,g0WO(dθ,dθ)=(dθ)24𝔼q[Y12+Y22]=12(dθ)2,∫Eq∥v0,dθ∥2dy=(dθ)2.\begin{aligned} g_0^{\mathrm{FR}}(d\theta,d\theta) &=(d\theta)^2\mathbb E_q[Y_1^2Y_2^2]=(d\theta)^2,\\ g_0^{\mathrm{WO}}(d\theta,d\theta) &=\frac{(d\theta)^2}{4}\mathbb E_q[Y_1^2+Y_2^2] =\frac12(d\theta)^2,\\ \int_Eq\|v_{0,d\theta}\|^2\,dy&=(d\theta)^2. \end{aligned} The raw velocity overestimates the WO squared norm by a factor of two; its equality with the Fisher cost is specific to this example. 8.6.0.6 5. Rapid spatial variation at a fixed velocity amplitude.

On the line, take a standard Gaussian reference and, for a fixed k>0k>0, the diffeomorphisms ϕθ(x)=x+θsin⁡(kx),|θ|k<1.\phi_\theta(x)=x+\theta\sin(kx), \qquad |\theta|k<1. At θ=0\theta=0, the velocity is v0,dθ(y)=dθsin⁡(ky)v_{0,d\theta}(y)=d\theta\sin(ky). It is already a spatial gradient. Writing v=v0,dθv=v_{0,d\theta} in the following identities, the continuity equation gives u(y)q(y)=yv(y)−v′(y),g0WO(dθ,dθ)=∫ℝq(y)v(y)2dy.\frac{u(y)}{q(y)}=y\,v(y)-v'(y), \qquad g_0^{\mathrm{WO}}(d\theta,d\theta)=\int_\mathbb{R}q(y)v(y)^2\,dy. For the standard Gaussian, integration by parts also gives g0FR(dθ,dθ)=∫ℝq(y)(v′(y)−yv(y))2dy=∫ℝq(y)(v′(y)2+v(y)2)dy.g_0^{\mathrm{FR}}(d\theta,d\theta) =\int_\mathbb{R}q(y)(v'(y)-yv(y))^2\,dy =\int_\mathbb{R}q(y)\bigl(v'(y)^2+v(y)^2\bigr)\,dy. 8.7 Geodesics

8.7.0.1 Translation with fixed shape.

For the translation family pμ(y)=q(y−μ)p_\mu(y)=q(y-\mu) considered above, both matrices are constant in the translation parameter μ\mu, so straight mean paths are intrinsic geodesics of this fixed-shape family, with different lengths. Allowing the covariance to vary gives a larger family and can change the Fisher–Rao minimizing path, as in Section 6.4.

8.7.0.2 Dilation of an isotropic Gaussian.

For pσ=𝒩(0,σ2In)p_\sigma=\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2I_n), the two metrics give 2n(dσ/σ)22n(d\sigma/\sigma)^2 and n(dσ)2n(d\sigma)^2, respectively. In this one-parameter family, the constant-speed geodesics are: σFR(t)=σ01−tσ1t,σWO(t)=(1−t)σ0+tσ1.\sigma_{\mathrm{FR}}(t)=\sigma_0^{1-t}\sigma_1^t, \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{WO}}(t)=(1-t)\sigma_0+t\sigma_1. The FR geodesic allocates equal time to equal logarithmic scale changes; the WO geodesic allocates equal time to equal absolute scale changes. The respective distances are 2n|log⁡(σ1/σ0)|\sqrt{2n}\,|\log(\sigma_1/\sigma_0)| and n|σ1−σ0|\sqrt n\,|\sigma_1-\sigma_0|. The first two columns of Figure 28 show this comparison for n=2n=2, σ0=1\sigma_0=1, and σ1=2\sigma_1=2: the two paths traverse the same densities with different time parameterizations.

8.7.0.3 Geodesics in the general parameter space.

For either pullback matrix G(θ)G(\theta), the energy of a parameter path is ℰg[θ]=12∫01θ̇(t)TG(θ(t))θ̇(t)dt.\mathcal E_g[\theta] =\frac12\int_0^1 \dot\theta(t)^TG(\theta(t))\dot\theta(t)\,dt. Intrinsic geodesics are stationary paths of this energy, with the coordinate equations given in Section 7.4; minimizing paths determine the intrinsic distance. Along such a path, Fisher–Rao penalizes the relative density rate, while Wasserstein–Otto penalizes the gradient component of vθ(t),θ̇(t)v_{\theta(t),\dot\theta(t)}. The resulting geodesics generally differ, and neither the parameter path nor the path of transport maps is necessarily straight.

For Wasserstein–Otto, there is a useful special case. Given endpoint maps ϕθ0\phi_{\theta_0} and ϕθ1\phi_{\theta_1}, the common reference law defines a coupling by transporting the same reference point to both endpoints. It gives the bound W22(pθ0,pθ1)≤∫Eq(x)∥ϕθ1(x)−ϕθ0(x)∥2dx.W_2^2(p_{\theta_0},p_{\theta_1}) \leq\int_Eq(x) \|\phi_{\theta_1}(x)-\phi_{\theta_0}(x)\|^2\,dx. If this coupling is optimal, its displacement interpolation is the ambient Wasserstein geodesic [19]. To realize it within the chosen parameterization, a sufficient condition is the existence of a path θ(t)∈Θ\theta(t)\in\Theta such that ϕθ(t)(x)=(1−t)ϕθ0(x)+tϕθ1(x),0≤t≤1.\phi_{\theta(t)}(x) =(1-t)\phi_{\theta_0}(x)+t\phi_{\theta_1}(x), \qquad 0\leq t\leq1. Under both conditions, this path also realizes the intrinsic distance. Optimality of the coupling and membership in the family are separate requirements. Membership likewise determines whether the ambient Fisher–Rao geodesic in square-root coordinates realizes an intrinsic geodesic.

The third column of Figure 28 illustrates why optimality of the endpoint coupling matters. For q=𝒩(0,I2)q=\mathcal N(0,I_2), the maps x↦2xx\mapsto2x and x↦2Rπ/2xx\mapsto2R_{\pi/2}x both produce 𝒩(0,4I2)\mathcal N(0,4I_2), where Rπ/2R_{\pi/2} is a quarter-turn. 9 Conclusion and perspectives

For a density variation uu, the two geometries studied in this atlas measure different aspects of the same change: gpFR(u,u)=∫Eu2p2pdx,gpWO(u,u)=∫Ep∥v∥2dx,u=−div⁡(pv),g_p^{\mathrm{FR}}(u,u) =\int_E\frac{u^2}{p^2}\,p\,dx, \qquad g_p^{\mathrm{WO}}(u,u) =\int_E p\|v\|^2\,dx, \qquad u=-\operatorname{div}(pv), where, in the WO expression, vv is the minimum-energy velocity realizing uu, hence the appropriate gradient field. Fisher–Rao measures relative density change; Wasserstein–Otto measures the motion of probability mass through the sample space. A fixed translation is therefore more costly for a narrow Gaussian under FR, but has the same WO cost whatever its width. Rapid spatial oscillations of a fixed-amplitude velocity can increase the FR cost without making the WO cost unbounded. Density-preserving motions cost nothing in either geometry, even when a chosen transport map moves particles. Pulling these metrics back to a family retains these distinctions, while constraining the admissible variations and geodesics to that family.

These differences suggest concrete questions for nonlinear optimization and machine learning. Given finitely many samples from an unknown density, one may fit pθp_\theta by minimizing an empirical loss ℒ(θ)\mathcal L(\theta). Where the pullback matrix G(θ)G(\theta) is positive definite, its natural gradient flow is [20] θ̇=−G(θ)−1∇θℒ(θ).\dot\theta=-G(\theta)^{-1}\nabla_\theta\mathcal L(\theta). The metric determines which density changes count as small optimization steps: FR uses relative change, whereas WO uses transport effort [12]. For the same loss, these choices can change the trajectory and conditioning, but not the stationary points where GG is invertible. With few samples, it is natural to ask how this choice interacts with model restrictions, regularization and early stopping. Neither metric alone resolves the underdetermination of density estimation, and a cheaper tangent direction does not by itself imply better generalization.

A further perspective is to choose a geometry through its symmetries. Both pullbacks are intrinsic under changes of parameter coordinates; this property does not distinguish FR from WO. The distinction concerns transformations of the sample space: FR is invariant under smooth invertible changes of observations, whereas WO preserves costs under isometries of its fixed ground metric. Diffeomorphism invariance leads to uniqueness results for FR under suitable hypotheses [3]; isometry invariance alone does not characterize WO. This suggests studying geometries adapted to other transformation groups or additional structure, such as anisotropic transport costs. A concrete example is covariance-modulated optimal transport [21], whose covariance-dependent Mahalanobis cost yields an affine-invariant transport geometry on distributions with finite, positive-definite covariance. Which symmetries should be preserved, which density changes should be expensive, and which computations remain feasible are then linked questions in the design of a learning method.
